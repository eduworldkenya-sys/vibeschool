# Chapter 2 — League of Nations

Curriculum: Form 4 History & Government → 24.0.0 World Wars → 24.2.2 League of Nations
Objectives: 24.1.0(d) formation; 24.1.0(e) organisation; 24.1.0(f) performance.
Status: DRAFT — requires authority/fact/pedagogy/exam/editor/critic gates before certification.

## Opening problem
The First World War killed millions and destabilised societies and economies. When fighting ended, statesmen faced a problem: how could another general European war be prevented? The League of Nations was one answer. Understanding it requires more than memorising organs: learners must connect why it was formed, how it was designed to work, what it achieved, where it failed, and why those weaknesses mattered by the 1930s.

## Learner outcomes
Learner should be able to explain the formation of the League; describe its organisation and functions; assess achievements and failures; explain reasons for weak performance; and construct evidence-based KCSE-style responses.

## Prerequisite bridge
Recall WWI results and the peace settlement. The devastation of war and the search for collective security provide the bridge to League formation.

# Lesson A — Formation

## Context and idea of collective security
The League emerged from wartime/post-war proposals for a permanent international organisation. US President Woodrow Wilson championed an association of nations in his Fourteen Points. The League Covenant became part of the post-war peace settlement and the League began work in 1920 with headquarters in Geneva.

Collective security meant that aggression against one member should concern the wider international community. The idea depended on states cooperating rather than treating every crisis as a private dispute.

## Why it was formed
Teaching categories:
1. prevent another major war through peaceful settlement and collective action;
2. encourage disarmament/reduction of armaments;
3. promote international cooperation;
4. supervise responsibilities created by the peace settlement, including mandates;
5. improve humanitarian, labour, health and social conditions through international cooperation.

## Formation chronology
1918: Wilson publicly sets out Fourteen Points.
1919: Paris Peace Conference; Covenant incorporated into peace settlement.
1920: League begins operations; Geneva becomes headquarters.

## Misconception
`The League was created only to punish Germany.` Correction: it grew out of the peace settlement, but its stated international role included peace/security and broad cooperation. Germany was initially outside the organisation and joined later.

## Formative checks
1. What problem was collective security intended to solve?
2. Explain one reason why an international organisation appeared attractive after WWI.
3. Distinguish the League from the Treaty of Versailles: they were connected but not identical.

## Teacher emphasis
Do not teach formation as a date list. Connect WWI destruction → peace settlement → collective-security problem → institutional response.

# Lesson B — Organisation

## Core organs
### Assembly
A broad deliberative body in which member states were represented. It discussed matters within the League's sphere, admitted members subject to rules, dealt with budget and elections/appointments in conjunction with other organs where required. Many important decisions required unanimity, which could slow action.

### Council
A smaller body responsible especially for urgent political/security matters. Its membership combined permanent and non-permanent representation over time. It dealt with disputes and recommendations/action under the Covenant.

### Secretariat
Permanent administrative machinery headquartered in Geneva, led by the Secretary-General. It prepared meetings, records, reports and day-to-day international administrative work.

### Permanent Court of International Justice
Based at The Hague. It dealt with legal disputes submitted by states and gave advisory opinions within its competence. Learners should distinguish judicial settlement from political decision-making in Assembly/Council.

### International Labour Organisation
Worked on labour conditions and social justice through an international tripartite structure involving governments, employers and workers. It was associated with the League system but had its own institutional character.

## Supporting commissions/activities
The League also worked through commissions and organisations concerned with mandates, refugees, health, trafficking/slavery and other international social/humanitarian problems. Exact institutional labels and periods require source-led verification during fact gate.

## How the structure was supposed to work
Dispute → investigation/discussion/legal settlement where appropriate → recommendation/collective response. The system depended heavily on member cooperation and willingness to enforce decisions.

## Misconceptions
- `The Court commanded armies.` No: it was a judicial institution.
- `The League had its own standing army.` No: enforcement depended on member states.
- `All organs did the same job.` No: deliberative, executive/political, administrative, judicial and specialised functions differed.

## Activity
Give learners organ cards and function cards. They match them, then explain why confusing Council with Secretariat changes understanding of League performance.

## Visual brief
A simple organisation diagram: League → Assembly / Council / Secretariat / PCIJ / ILO / commissions. Visual must distinguish core League organs from associated/specialised bodies accurately after fact review.

# Lesson C — Performance

## How to judge performance
Performance should not be reduced to `successes vs failures`. Ask:
- Did the League settle disputes?
- Did states obey its decisions?
- Could it deter powerful aggressors?
- Did it improve humanitarian/social cooperation?
- Were failures caused by design, membership, great-power politics, economic conditions, or several factors together?

## Areas of achievement
The League contributed to peaceful settlement/management of a number of disputes involving smaller powers, supported refugee and humanitarian work, developed health cooperation, addressed trafficking/slavery and labour concerns through international mechanisms, and administered/supervised mandate-related responsibilities. Specific case studies (for example Åland Islands and Upper Silesia) must be fact-checked with exact chronology and role before certification.

## Why early successes could be misleading
Many successful interventions occurred where major powers were willing to cooperate and their core interests were not directly threatened. That reveals a central limitation: institutional rules alone could not substitute for political commitment.

## Major weaknesses/failures
### Absence or inconsistent participation of major powers
The United States never joined. Other major powers entered, withdrew, were excluded or were absent during important periods. This weakened universality and credibility.

### Unanimity and slow decision-making
Consensus requirements made decisive action difficult, particularly in fast-moving crises.

### No standing military force
The League relied on member states to apply sanctions or military pressure. Governments could be unwilling to bear those costs.

### Economic sanctions could be incomplete
Sanctions required broad cooperation and could fail when important states or commodities/routes were outside measures.

### Great-power self-interest
Members often placed national strategic interests above collective security.

### Aggression in the 1930s
Crises involving Japanese expansion in Manchuria and Italian aggression against Ethiopia exposed the League's inability to compel powerful aggressors effectively. Germany's expansion and the wider collapse of collective security further demonstrated the weakness of the interwar system. Each crisis requires detailed chronology during full content/fact expansion.

### Wider environment
The Great Depression intensified nationalism, protectionism, political extremism and reluctance to accept international costs, making cooperation harder.

## Ethiopia/Abyssinia teaching case
Use this as a causal case study, not merely a failure bullet:
Italian aggression → League condemnation/sanctions → sanctions limited/incomplete and member interests constrain action → aggression succeeds → credibility of collective security damaged.
Exact dates, sanction scope and diplomatic episodes require source ledger verification before certification.

## Manchuria teaching case
Japanese military action → League investigation/deliberation → delayed/inadequate coercive response → Japan rejects League pressure/withdraws → demonstrates time/enforcement problem. Exact sequence to be fact-checked before certification.

## Historical judgement
A strong answer should avoid `League did nothing`. It achieved meaningful humanitarian and technical cooperation and some dispute settlement, but proved unable to uphold collective security against determined major-power aggression. Its effectiveness depended on political cooperation that weakened severely in the 1930s.

# Think Like a Historian
Which mattered more in League failure: institutional design or member-state choices? Build an argument using at least three pieces of evidence and acknowledge one counterargument.

# Teacher notes
Key teaching move: formation → organisation → performance must be causal. Ask learners after each organ: `How could this design help peace? How could it limit action?`
Board summary: `Purpose → Machinery → Achievements → Limits → 1930s crises → judgement`.
Expected learner difficulty: memorising organs without functions; treating all failures as one cause; assuming the League's failure was inevitable from 1920.

# Learner quick notes
Formation: post-WWI peace problem; Covenant; Geneva; collective security; cooperation/disarmament/humanitarian aims.
Organisation: Assembly, Council, Secretariat, PCIJ, ILO and commissions/specialised work.
Achievements: selected dispute settlement + humanitarian/health/labour cooperation.
Weaknesses: incomplete great-power participation, unanimity, no standing force, sanctions/enforcement limits, self-interest, 1930s aggression and hostile economic/political environment.
Judgement: useful cooperation, weak coercion against powerful aggressors.

# Practice assessment
## Recall
Name the city where League headquarters were located.

## Understanding
Explain the meaning of collective security.

## Structure
Describe the role of the Secretariat and distinguish it from the Council.

## KCSE-style VibeSchool practice
Explain five reasons for the failure of the League of Nations to maintain international peace. (10 marks candidate format; exact marking contract pending examiner gate.)

Indicative defensible areas: absence/inconsistent participation of major powers; unanimity/slow decisions; lack of standing force; dependence on members for sanctions/enforcement; national self-interest; weak/incomplete sanctions; economic/political conditions; inability to deter major-power aggression. Final marking scheme must avoid double-crediting overlapping explanations.

## Strong-answer model pattern
`Factor → historical mechanism → consequence for League effectiveness.` Example: lack of an independent military force meant the League depended on member governments to supply coercive power; when governments were unwilling to risk forces or strategic interests, League decisions could not reliably stop aggression.

## Weak-answer diagnosis
`The League failed because it was weak.` This merely restates the outcome. Improve by identifying what made it weak and showing how that prevented effective collective action.

# Revision retrieval
Without notes: list five League institutions/associated bodies; explain collective security in one sentence; name two structural/political weaknesses; give one area of achievement; explain why a smaller-state success does not prove the League could deter a major aggressor.
