## Opening problem
After WWI, governments knew that the old balance-of-power system had failed catastrophically. The League of Nations attempted something ambitious: make peace an organised international responsibility. Its story is therefore not simply `an organisation that failed`. It is a test of whether rules and institutions can keep peace when powerful states are unwilling to accept the costs of collective action.

## Learner outcomes
Explain why/how the League formed; describe its organisation and functions; explain collective security; assess political, humanitarian and technical achievements; analyse Manchuria and Ethiopia as case studies; explain structural/political reasons for failure; write developed KCSE-style answers.

# A. Formation

## From WWI destruction to a new idea
WWI demonstrated how alliances, arms competition and crisis escalation could turn a dispute into mass war. Peace planners therefore considered a permanent international body in which states could discuss disputes before resorting to force. US President Woodrow Wilson made an association of nations a prominent part of his Fourteen Points in January 1918, although ideas for international cooperation had earlier roots.

At the Paris Peace Conference in 1919, the Covenant of the League of Nations was drafted and incorporated into the Treaty of Versailles and related peace arrangements. The League formally came into existence in January 1920 and operated from Geneva.

## Collective security
Collective security is easier to understand through a classroom example. If every country treats an aggressor's attack as somebody else's problem, an aggressor can isolate victims one at a time. Collective security attempts to reverse that calculation: aggression should bring a wider response from the international community.

For that to work, states must agree on what counts as aggression, respond quickly and be willing to impose diplomatic/economic—and potentially military—costs. This dependency on political will later became a central weakness.

## Main purposes
- preserve peace through peaceful dispute settlement and collective response;
- promote international cooperation;
- encourage reduction/control of armaments;
- uphold aspects of the post-war settlement;
- supervise mandates;
- improve labour, health, refugee and other humanitarian/social conditions.

## Membership problem from the beginning
Wilson was a major advocate, but the United States never joined because the US Senate did not approve participation. Germany was initially excluded and joined in 1926 before later withdrawing; the Soviet Union joined much later, in 1934, and was expelled in 1939. Japan and Italy also left the League after conflict with it. The changing membership meant the organisation was never a stable assembly of all major powers.

**Why this matters:** collective security is strongest when states capable of enforcing it participate consistently.

## Formation timeline
1918 Fourteen Points → 1919 Paris Peace Conference/Covenant → Jan 1920 League begins → later membership changes reshape its strength.

# B. Organisation

## Assembly
The Assembly represented the member states and met regularly. It discussed issues within the League's competence, dealt with membership and budgetary matters and participated in elections/appointments under League procedures. Each member state had representation, but important decisions often required unanimity.

**Strength:** broad international deliberation.
**Weakness:** unanimity could make decisive agreement difficult.

## Council
The Council was the smaller political/security body and dealt especially with disputes and threats to peace. Permanent and non-permanent membership changed over time. Because security crises could require rapid decisions, the Council was central to collective-security performance.

**Key distinction:** Assembly = broad deliberation; Council = smaller security/political machinery. Neither was a world government with automatic coercive power.

## Secretariat
The Secretariat was the permanent administrative service in Geneva under the Secretary-General. It organised meetings, prepared documents/reports, maintained records and supported the continuing work of the organisation.

**Teaching analogy:** if Assembly/Council make political decisions, the Secretariat provides institutional memory and administration needed for those decisions to become organised work.

## Permanent Court of International Justice
Located at The Hague, the Court provided a judicial route for disputes submitted to it and advisory opinions within its competence. It illustrates the League-era attempt to substitute legal settlement for force in suitable disputes.

## International Labour Organisation
Created in the post-war settlement and connected to the League system, the ILO promoted improved labour standards through cooperation among governments, employers and workers. Its tripartite design was distinctive and the organisation survived the League era.

## Mandates system
Former German colonies and former Ottoman territories were placed under mandates administered by designated powers under international supervision. The system claimed a supervisory/international responsibility, but in practice retained colonial control and did not amount to immediate self-government. Learners should be able to evaluate both the stated principle and imperial reality.

## Humanitarian and technical bodies
League work extended to refugees, public health, trafficking, slavery, drugs, economic/financial cooperation and intellectual cooperation. This matters because judging the League only by whether it stopped WWII erases areas where international cooperation produced lasting institutional experience.

# C. Performance — successes

## Peaceful dispute settlement
The League helped manage several disputes where the parties were willing to accept mediation/arbitration and where vital great-power interests were limited.

### Åland Islands
Finland and Sweden disputed the strategically located, Swedish-speaking Åland Islands after Finland's independence. The League considered the dispute and in 1921 supported Finnish sovereignty while providing protections for the islands' Swedish language/culture and reinforcing demilitarised status. Both states accepted the settlement.

**Lesson:** League machinery could work when states accepted international mediation and enforcement did not require confronting a determined major aggressor.

### Upper Silesia
Germany and Poland disputed Upper Silesia, an industrially important territory, after WWI. A plebiscite and subsequent conflict left the issue difficult. The League helped develop a territorial settlement and arrangements intended to manage economic/minority issues. The region was divided between Germany and Poland.

**Lesson:** the League could contribute to complicated negotiated settlements, but such successes occurred in a different strategic environment from the crises of the 1930s.

## Humanitarian/technical achievements
The League and associated institutions contributed to refugee assistance, health cooperation, labour standards, campaigns against trafficking/slavery and international administrative cooperation. The Nansen refugee system became particularly associated with assistance to stateless/refugee populations.

**Historical judgement:** these achievements did not compensate for security failure, but they show why `the League achieved nothing` is historically wrong.

# D. Performance — failures and crises

## Manchurian Crisis, 1931–33
### What happened
In September 1931, following the Mukden Incident, Japanese forces occupied Manchuria in northeastern China. Japan subsequently supported creation of Manchukuo. China appealed to the League.

### League response
The League sent the Lytton Commission to investigate. Investigation and deliberation took many months. The resulting League position did not accept the Japanese-created political settlement as legitimate in the way Japan demanded.

### Outcome
Japan rejected the League's judgement and announced withdrawal from the organisation. The League did not impose coercive measures capable of reversing the occupation.

### Why the case mattered
The crisis exposed three weaknesses: **speed**, because investigation was slow relative to military action; **enforcement**, because condemnation did not compel withdrawal; and **great-power commitment**, because members were unwilling to undertake the risks/costs of stronger action in East Asia.

## Abyssinian/Ethiopian Crisis, 1935–36
### Background
Fascist Italy under Benito Mussolini sought imperial expansion. Ethiopia (then commonly called Abyssinia in European sources) was a League member and one of the few independent African states.

### Invasion and League response
Italy invaded Ethiopia in October 1935. The League identified Italy as the aggressor and members imposed economic/financial sanctions. But sanctions were incomplete: crucially, oil was not included, and the Suez Canal remained open to Italian shipping. Britain and France also had strategic reasons for avoiding a complete break with Mussolini.

### Outcome
Italian forces captured Addis Ababa in May 1936 and Mussolini proclaimed annexation. League sanctions had failed to save a member from conquest.

### Why this was devastating
Collective security had promised that aggression would become a common concern. Ethiopia demonstrated that when enforcement threatened major-power strategic interests, members could choose limited action. The failure damaged the League's credibility far beyond Africa.

### African significance
For African learners this is not a peripheral European diplomatic episode. An African member state appealed to an international collective-security system and was conquered while that system failed to stop the aggressor. The crisis became an important symbol for Africans and people of African descent concerned with colonialism, racism and international power.

## Disarmament failure
Disarmament was one of the League-era aspirations, but states disagreed over security and equality of armaments. The World Disarmament Conference opened in 1932 amid worsening international tension and ultimately failed to create an effective general disarmament settlement. German withdrawal from the conference/League and subsequent rearmament demonstrated the deteriorating environment.

# E. Why did the League fail to maintain peace?

## 1. United States absence
The world's increasingly important economic power remained outside. This weakened universality and complicated sanctions/collective pressure.

## 2. Changing/partial great-power membership
Germany, USSR, Japan and Italy were absent, late entrants, withdrawers or expelled at different points. A collective system without consistent major-power participation had limited reach.

## 3. Dependence on unanimity/consensus
Decision procedures could delay or weaken action. Security crises often move faster than diplomatic consensus.

## 4. No standing League armed force
The League depended on member governments. A resolution did not automatically produce troops or coercive capacity.

## 5. Weakness of sanctions
Economic sanctions required broad participation and careful targeting. Partial sanctions could hurt but fail to stop aggression; military enforcement carried risks governments often rejected.

## 6. National self-interest
Britain, France and other states frequently balanced League obligations against imperial, economic and strategic interests. Collective security was therefore conditional on national calculations.

## 7. Great Depression and political extremism
Economic crisis weakened international cooperation, encouraged protectionism and contributed to political radicalisation. Fascist/Nazi/militarist regimes were increasingly willing to challenge the settlement.

## 8. Aggressors learned from earlier weakness
Failure to reverse Manchuria and Ethiopia suggested that international condemnation might not be followed by decisive enforcement. This weakened deterrence.

## 9. League connected to an unpopular post-war order
Some states associated the League with the Versailles settlement and believed the existing international order was unfair. Revisionist powers challenged both territorial arrangements and the institutions supporting them.

# F. Balanced judgement
The League was neither useless nor capable of doing what its strongest promises implied. It developed important forms of international administration, humanitarian cooperation and peaceful settlement. But collective security required powerful states to act together against aggression. When that cooperation collapsed, the League lacked independent power to substitute for it.

This distinction prepares learners for the next chapter: WWII was not caused simply because `the League was weak`; rather, League weakness interacted with aggressive expansion, economic/political instability, appeasement, rearmament and specific state decisions.
