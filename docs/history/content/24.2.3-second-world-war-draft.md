# Chapter 3 — Second World War (1939–1945)

## Opening problem
WWI was supposed to be the war that forced governments to build a safer international order. Instead, by September 1939 Europe was at war again. WWII is best understood as a chain: unresolved post-WWI grievances and economic instability created opportunities; authoritarian regimes pursued expansion; collective security failed; other powers repeatedly calculated that concessions were safer than confrontation; finally the invasion of Poland produced war between Germany, Britain and France.

# A. Causes

## Versailles and revisionism
Germany lost territory, faced military restrictions and reparations obligations under the post-WWI settlement. Many Germans regarded aspects of Versailles as humiliating/unjust, and Nazi propaganda exploited this resentment. But Versailles is a background condition, not a mechanical cause: Germany did not automatically have to become Nazi or start another war.

## Great Depression and political instability
The Depression produced unemployment, insecurity and political radicalisation across many societies. In Germany it helped undermine confidence in democratic government and strengthened extremist parties. Internationally, economic nationalism/protectionism made cooperation harder. Economic crisis therefore created conditions in which aggressive politics became easier to mobilise.

## Fascism, Nazism and militarist expansion
Mussolini's Italy sought imperial prestige and conquered Ethiopia. Nazi Germany combined dictatorship, racial ideology, anti-communism, rearmament and territorial expansion. Japan's military expansion in Manchuria/China challenged collective security in Asia. These were not identical regimes, but each contributed to erosion of the international order.

## Hitler's foreign-policy programme
Hitler sought to overturn Versailles restrictions, rearm Germany, unite German-speaking populations under German rule and expand German territory, especially eastward. Nazi racial ideology made this expansion far more than conventional border revision.

## League failure
Manchuria and Ethiopia showed that the League could condemn aggression without necessarily reversing it. Aggressive states could therefore question whether collective security would impose decisive costs.

## Rearmament and Rhineland
Germany openly rearmed and reintroduced conscription in violation of Versailles restrictions. In March 1936 German forces entered the demilitarised Rhineland. Britain and France did not use force to reverse the move. Hitler gained prestige and Germany's strategic position improved.

## Appeasement — why governments used it
Appeasement means attempting to settle grievances through negotiation/concessions rather than immediate military confrontation. British/French policymakers were influenced by traumatic memories of WWI, economic/military constraints, uncertainty over public support, concern about communism among some leaders, and a belief that some German grievances could be revised peacefully.

**Historical-thinking point:** judging appeasement only with knowledge of what happened later is hindsight. Learners should understand why policymakers considered it rational and then evaluate its consequences.

## Anschluss, 1938
German pressure and intervention culminated in Austria's incorporation into Nazi Germany in March 1938. The Anschluss violated the post-war settlement and strengthened Germany materially/strategically.

## Sudeten crisis and Munich
Hitler demanded the Sudetenland, a border region of Czechoslovakia with a large German-speaking population. At the Munich Conference in September 1938, Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed that the Sudetenland would pass to Germany; Czechoslovakia was not an equal party to the decision. British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain hoped the settlement would preserve peace.

In March 1939 German forces occupied the Czech lands, demonstrating that Hitler's ambitions extended beyond simply uniting German-speaking territories. This severely damaged confidence in appeasement.

## Nazi-Soviet Pact
On 23 August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression pact. A secret protocol divided spheres of influence in Eastern Europe. For Hitler, the agreement reduced the immediate danger of Soviet intervention while Germany attacked Poland; for Stalin it bought time and served Soviet strategic interests.

## Poland — immediate trigger
Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Britain and France, which had guaranteed Polish independence, declared war on Germany on 3 September. The invasion was the immediate trigger of the European war, while the causes above explain why the international system had reached that point.

## Cause web
Versailles grievances + Depression → extremist opportunity; Nazi ideology/expansion + rearmament → growing threat; League weakness + appeasement → inadequate deterrence; Anschluss/Czechoslovakia → escalating expansion; Nazi-Soviet Pact → reduced two-front risk; Poland → immediate outbreak.

# B. Course

## 1939: Poland and Blitzkrieg
German forces attacked Poland using coordinated armour, aircraft, artillery and mobile forces. The term `Blitzkrieg` is commonly used for this rapid operational style. The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September under the German-Soviet arrangements. Poland was defeated and partitioned.

## 1939–40: `Phoney War`, Scandinavia and Western Europe
For several months little major land fighting occurred on the Franco-German frontier. Germany then invaded Denmark and Norway in April 1940, securing strategic positions and access connected to Swedish iron-ore routes.

In May, Germany attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and France. German forces broke through the Ardennes and reached the Channel, cutting off Allied forces. Large numbers of Allied troops were evacuated from Dunkirk. France signed an armistice in June 1940; northern/western areas were occupied while the Vichy regime governed from the south under German dominance.

## Battle of Britain, 1940
Britain remained at war. Germany sought air superiority as a prerequisite for a possible invasion. The Luftwaffe attacked RAF infrastructure and later increasingly bombed cities during the Blitz. Radar, Britain's integrated air-defence system, fighter aircraft, pilots from Britain/Commonwealth/occupied Europe and fighting over home territory contributed to British resistance. Germany failed to gain the air superiority necessary for invasion.

**Significance:** Hitler had failed to eliminate Britain; Germany now faced a continuing western enemy.

## North Africa and Mediterranean
Italy entered the war in June 1940. Fighting in North Africa centred on Egypt, Libya, the Suez route and wider Mediterranean strategy. Germany sent the Afrika Korps under Erwin Rommel to support Italy. British/Commonwealth/Allied forces repeatedly advanced and retreated across the desert.

At the Second Battle of El Alamein in late 1942, forces under Bernard Montgomery defeated the Axis advance in Egypt. Allied landings in Morocco/Algeria (Operation Torch) put additional pressure on Axis forces, which surrendered in North Africa in 1943.

## Operation Barbarossa, June 1941
Germany invaded the Soviet Union in the largest land invasion of the war. German forces made enormous early advances, but the Soviet Union did not collapse. Distance, logistics, Soviet resistance, mobilisation and severe fighting frustrated a quick victory. The Eastern Front became the largest and bloodiest theatre of the European war.

## Pearl Harbor and globalisation, December 1941
Japan attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941. The United States declared war on Japan; Germany and Italy then declared war on the United States. The conflict was now unmistakably global, linking European, Mediterranean, Asian and Pacific theatres.

## Pacific expansion and Midway
Japan rapidly captured territories across Southeast Asia and the Pacific. At the Battle of Midway in June 1942, US forces sank four Japanese fleet carriers, severely damaging Japan's carrier striking strength. Midway helped shift strategic initiative in the Pacific, though years of hard fighting remained.

## Stalingrad, 1942–43
German forces advanced toward the Caucasus and Stalingrad. Intense urban combat followed. Soviet forces encircled the German Sixth Army in November 1942; it surrendered in early 1943. Stalingrad destroyed a major German force, damaged Axis prestige and marked a major strategic turning point as Soviet forces increasingly took the initiative.

## Italy, 1943
After victory in North Africa, Allied forces invaded Sicily and then mainland Italy. Mussolini was removed from power, and the Italian government sought an armistice with the Allies. Germany occupied much of Italy and fighting continued up the peninsula. Italy therefore did not simply `leave the war` in one clean moment.

## D-Day and liberation of Western Europe
On 6 June 1944 Allied forces landed in Normandy in Operation Overlord. A beachhead was established, forces broke out into France and Paris was liberated in August. Germany now faced powerful Allied armies from the west while Soviet armies advanced from the east.

## Soviet advance and Germany's defeat
The Soviet Union launched enormous offensives that drove German forces westward through Eastern Europe. By 1945 Allied armies entered Germany. Hitler died in Berlin at the end of April. Germany surrendered unconditionally in May 1945; 8 May is commemorated as VE Day in much of Europe.

## Pacific endgame
US forces advanced across the Pacific through island campaigns while submarine warfare and bombing weakened Japan. Fierce battles such as Iwo Jima and Okinawa demonstrated the likely cost of continued fighting.

The United States dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and another on Nagasaki on 9 August. The Soviet Union declared war on Japan and invaded Japanese-held territory in Manchuria in August. Japan announced acceptance of surrender terms on 15 August; formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945.

**Historical caution:** explain Japan's surrender as a debated multi-factor outcome involving atomic bombing, Soviet entry, military/economic collapse and Japanese decision-making; avoid presenting complex causation as a single unquestionable sentence.

## Africa and East Africa
African territories supplied soldiers, labour, food and strategic resources. East African troops served in campaigns including East Africa and later other theatres under British imperial command. The war also altered colonial economies and exposed African servicemen/workers to new political experiences. Specific Kenya examples and numbers must be source-verified during certification rather than invented for local flavour.

# C. Results

## Human catastrophe
WWII killed tens of millions of people, with civilians forming a huge proportion. Combat, bombing, starvation, disease, occupation policies, forced labour and mass murder devastated populations. Exact totals differ by methodology and must use sourced ranges.

## Holocaust and genocide
Nazi Germany and its collaborators systematically persecuted and murdered six million European Jews during the Holocaust, alongside the persecution/murder of Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Poles, political opponents and other targeted groups. Teaching this as merely `many people died` destroys its historical meaning: genocide was organised state policy rooted in Nazi racial ideology.

## Refugees/displacement
Borders shifted, populations fled advancing armies, prisoners and forced labourers had to return or resettle, and Holocaust survivors often had no homes/communities to return to. Europe and parts of Asia faced enormous displacement crises.

## Economic and physical destruction
Cities, factories, farms, railways, ports and housing were destroyed. European and Asian economies required major reconstruction. Wartime mobilisation also expanded industrial capacity in countries such as the United States.

## United Nations
The failure of interwar collective security and experience of WWII encouraged creation of a new organisation. Representatives of 50 countries met in San Francisco in 1945 to draft/sign the UN Charter; the United Nations formally came into existence on 24 October 1945. Unlike the League system, the new security structure gave special responsibility and veto power to five permanent Security Council members.

## Superpowers and Cold War
European powers were weakened while the United States and Soviet Union emerged as the dominant military/political powers. Their ideological and strategic rivalry rapidly shaped post-war Europe and international relations, producing the Cold War.

## Germany and Europe divided
Germany was occupied by the victorious powers and divided into occupation zones; Berlin was similarly divided. These arrangements later developed into separate West and East German states. Eastern European governments increasingly came under Soviet influence, while Western Europe aligned increasingly with the United States.

## Decolonisation
European imperial states had been weakened financially and militarily. Colonial soldiers/workers had contributed to victory, while wartime rhetoric about freedom and self-determination created obvious contradictions with colonial rule. Nationalist movements grew stronger in Asia and Africa. WWII accelerated decolonisation but did not alone cause it; local nationalist organisation and longer colonial histories remained essential.

## Africa/Kenya significance
The war intensified demands on African colonies for manpower, labour, food and resources. Returning servicemen and wartime economic/social change contributed to changing political expectations. In Kenya and elsewhere, these developments formed part of the wider background to post-war nationalism. Exact Kenya-specific claims must be tied to authoritative East African/Kenyan sources in certification.

## War-crimes trials
The Nuremberg and Tokyo trials prosecuted leading Axis officials. They strengthened international attention to individual responsibility for war crimes and crimes against humanity, although their legal/political legacy requires careful explanation.

## Nuclear age
The atomic bombings demonstrated a new level of destructive power. Nuclear weapons became central to post-war security strategy and Cold War deterrence, while also creating global movements and diplomacy concerned with nuclear control/disarmament.

## Technology and medicine
War accelerated radar, aviation, rocketry, computing/communications, logistics and medical innovation. Some technologies later had major civilian applications. Avoid claiming every post-war invention was `caused by WWII`; distinguish acceleration from invention.

## International economic reconstruction
Post-war governments sought to avoid the economic instability associated with the interwar period. New international financial arrangements and reconstruction programmes helped reshape the global economy. Detailed institutions can be developed under later topics where curriculum-relevant.

# D. Connections learners must understand
League failure → weak collective security → aggression/appeasement → WWII.
WWII destruction → UN.
WWII alliance against Axis → US-Soviet rivalry after common enemy disappears → Cold War.
Imperial mobilisation + weakened European empires + nationalist organisation → accelerated decolonisation.
Atomic bombs → nuclear age/Cold War strategic change.

- Turning-point ranking with evidence.
- Cause/result sorting to stop learners mixing command words.
- Compare League and UN security designs at introductory level.

## Misconceptions
`WWII began everywhere on the same date`; `France did not fight`; `Pearl Harbor started the European war`; `Russia and USSR are interchangeable in all periods`; `atomic bombs were the only factor in Japanese surrender`; `WWII instantly caused African independence`; `appeasement means support for Hitler`.
