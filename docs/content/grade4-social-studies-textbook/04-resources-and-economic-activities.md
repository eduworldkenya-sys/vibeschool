# Chapter 4 — Resources and economic activities

## 4.1 How people earn a living

Max walks through a local market and sees vegetables, baskets, shoes and people offering repair services. Behind each of these goods or services is someone's work. Some people grow or make things; others transport, sell or repair them.

### Resources in our county

A resource is something useful that people can use to meet a need. Natural resources include land, water, soil, forests and minerals. People also use their knowledge, skills, tools and machines to carry out work. Different counties have different resources and opportunities.

Economic activities are activities through which people produce, distribute or exchange goods and services to meet needs and earn a living. Farming produces food and other products. Fishing provides fish. Trading makes goods available to buyers. Transport moves people and goods. Tourism involves services for visitors, including guiding and accommodation. Craftwork and manufacturing turn materials into useful products.

A community with suitable soils and reliable water may grow crops. A community near a lake may have fishing activities. A busy town may have shops, workshops and transport services. A county may support many different activities at the same time.

### Why economic activities matter

Economic activities provide goods, services, jobs and income. Income helps families buy food, pay for transport and meet other needs. The goods and services produced by one group can help another group carry out its work.

For example, a carpenter uses timber, tools and skills to make a table. A trader supplies the tools, a transport worker carries materials, and a customer buys the table. Each contributes to the activity.

Resources need care. Cutting trees without replacing them can harm forests and water catchment areas. Dumping waste into rivers can damage water resources. People can conserve resources by reducing waste, reusing suitable materials, planting appropriate trees and using water carefully.

Children can learn about economic activities through supervised gardening, craftwork, market simulations or interviews. Learning activities should be safe and age-appropriate; children must not be sent to work in dangerous conditions or miss school to earn money.

## 4.2 Industries in our county

Imagine two families buying yoghurt at a shop. Before reaching the shop, milk was collected, processed, packaged and transported. The finished product is the result of work carried out in a series of places.

### From raw material to product

An industry is an organised economic activity producing goods or providing services. Manufacturing and processing industries make or change products using materials, equipment and workers' skills. A raw material is an input used to make a product.

| Raw material | Example of industry | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Milk | Dairy processing | Yoghurt |
| Timber | Furniture making | Chair |
| Clay | Brick making | Bricks |
| Grain | Milling | Flour |

A cottage industry is a small-scale industry, sometimes operating in a home or small workshop. A large factory may use more workers, machinery and materials. Both can make useful products.

An industry may be located near raw materials, workers, reliable water, electricity, transport routes or customers. A milk-processing plant may need reliable transport from dairy farms. A furniture workshop may need a supply of timber from lawful and sustainable sources.

### Benefits and responsibilities

Industries provide jobs, goods and income. They can add value to raw materials, such as turning grain into flour, and support transport and trading businesses. County governments and communities can benefit from lawful business activity and appropriate services.

Industries must also manage waste, protect workers and reduce pollution. Factory machinery, chemicals, dust and hot surfaces can be dangerous. Children should visit industries only in an approved, supervised group and follow all safety instructions. Never operate equipment during a visit unless an appropriate safe learning activity has been authorised by the responsible adults.

## 4.3 Starting an enterprise project at school

Hadassa's class notices that many learners need bookmarks. The class decides to make bookmarks from clean card left over from other activities. They ask their teacher for permission, discuss what is needed and agree how to share the work.

### Choosing a useful project

An enterprise project is an organised activity that creates a useful good or service, sometimes for sale. Possible supervised school projects include seedlings, simple crafts, reusable bookmarks or a vegetable garden. A good project is safe, useful, affordable, suitable for the learners and possible with the available resources.

Before choosing, ask: Who needs the product? What materials do we have? How much will the materials cost? Who will do each task? How can we avoid waste and keep everyone safe?

### Planning, making and keeping records

The bookmark group plans its project. One team designs the bookmarks, another prepares clean materials, and another checks quality. The teacher supervises the use of scissors and all money handling. Learners who do not want to cut or draw can help with counting, organising or checking.

Here is an imaginary project record.

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Card and string | KSh 80 |
| Markers | KSh 40 |
| Total cost | KSh 120 |
| Money received from sales | KSh 160 |
| Amount remaining after costs | KSh 40 |

The difference between money received and project costs is KSh 160 − KSh 120 = KSh 40. This is the surplus in the simple example. If the money received is less than the costs, the project has a loss. Keep accurate records even when a project does not make money.

### Working together ethically

Ethics are principles of right conduct. In an enterprise, learners show ethics by being honest about costs and sales, treating customers fairly, sharing tasks fairly, caring for materials and reporting problems. No learner should be forced to pay, sell goods or take unsafe tasks.

A group can also make reflective paper designs or models of visibility aids to discuss road safety. A homemade classroom model is not certified protective equipment and must not be relied on in traffic.

### Our project plan

With your teacher, choose a safe project and make a plan: purpose, materials, cost estimate, roles, safety rules, record of work and how to judge the outcome. Make or model the product. Review what went well, what could improve and how the group treated its members. If learning alone, prepare a paper plan rather than handling real sales.
