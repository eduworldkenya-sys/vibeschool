## Start here
On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife Sophie were assassinated in Sarajevo. Within weeks, much of Europe was at war.

The assassination matters, but it does not by itself explain why a local crisis became a world war. Europe had accumulated rivalries, military plans, alliances and nationalist tensions for years. The assassination triggered a crisis inside an already dangerous international system.

**Guiding question:** Why did the Sarajevo assassination lead to a major European war instead of remaining a dispute between Austria-Hungary and Serbia?

## What you should understand
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain the major long-term causes of the First World War;
- distinguish underlying causes from the immediate trigger;

## Before 1914: Europe under pressure
There was no single cause of the First World War. Several developments increased mistrust and made a general war more likely. Nationalism sharpened rivalries and destabilised multinational empires. Imperial competition increased friction among powers. Military expansion made governments more prepared to use force. Alliances connected the security of one state to others. Repeated crises, especially in the Balkans, tested this tense system. Sarajevo then provided the immediate crisis in which these pressures interacted.

## Key vocabulary
**Nationalism** — strong identification with and loyalty to a nation, sometimes accompanied by a desire for political independence or national power.

**Imperialism** — extending a country's power or control over other territories or peoples.

**Militarism** — strong emphasis on military power and the belief that armed strength is an important means of protecting or advancing national interests.

**Alliance** — an agreement between states to cooperate, often for security or defence.

**Arms race** — competition between states to increase or improve military forces and weapons.

**Balkans** — a region of south-eastern Europe where nationalist and great-power rivalries were particularly intense before 1914.

**Immediate trigger** — the event that sets a crisis in motion; it is not necessarily the same as the deeper causes that made the crisis dangerous.

# The causes

## 1. Nationalism
Nationalism contributed to war in more than one way.

First, rivalry between established European states encouraged competition for status and power. France, for example, had lost Alsace-Lorraine to Germany after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871. The loss remained an important source of French resentment and Franco-German tension.

Second, nationalism was destabilising in the Balkans. The Austro-Hungarian Empire contained many national groups, while Serbian nationalism supported the idea of bringing South Slavs together. Austria-Hungary feared that Serbian influence could encourage nationalist movements among Slavic peoples inside its empire.

Nationalism therefore increased both rivalry between major powers and instability within multinational empires. In the Sarajevo crisis, the conflict between Austria-Hungary and Serbia was tied directly to these nationalist tensions.

**Check:** Why was nationalism in the Balkans a threat to Austria-Hungary?

## 2. Imperial rivalry
European powers competed for colonies, markets, strategic positions and international prestige. Competition overseas did not automatically cause war in Europe, but it contributed to suspicion and rivalry.

Germany's growing power and imperial ambitions challenged a system in which Britain and France already possessed large overseas empires. Crises over Morocco in 1905–1906 and 1911 intensified tension between Germany and France. These confrontations also helped strengthen cooperation among states that feared German power.

The important connection is this: imperial disputes deepened mistrust. When the 1914 crisis arrived, the major powers were not dealing with one another as neutral strangers; they already carried years of rivalry and suspicion.

**Exam connection:** Do not merely write "countries competed for colonies." Explain how that competition increased rivalry or hostility between powers.

## 3. Militarism and the arms race
Before 1914, the major powers expanded their armed forces and developed military plans for a possible European war. Military strength increasingly became central to national security and prestige.

The naval competition between Britain and Germany is a clear example. Germany expanded its navy, while Britain sought to preserve its naval superiority. The launch of HMS *Dreadnought* in 1906 accelerated competition in powerful modern battleships.

Large armies and detailed mobilisation plans also mattered. Once a crisis began, leaders faced pressure to mobilise quickly because military plans depended on speed. Mobilisation could itself appear threatening to rivals, encouraging them to mobilise in response.

Militarism therefore did more than provide weapons. It helped create an environment in which governments prepared for war and in which a political crisis could rapidly become a military emergency.

**Think:** Why can preparing for defence make another country feel less secure?

## 4. Alliance systems
By the years before 1914, Europe had developed two major groupings. The **Triple Alliance** linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy. The **Triple Entente** connected Britain, France and Russia through a series of agreements and understandings.

These arrangements were intended to improve security, but they also meant that a confrontation involving one power could affect the calculations of several others. Austria-Hungary could look toward Germany; Serbia could expect sympathy and support from Russia; France was allied with Russia; Britain was increasingly concerned about the European balance of power and had understandings with France and Russia.

It is too simple to say that alliances "automatically" forced every country into war. Governments still made decisions. Their alliances, strategic commitments and expectations, however, widened the crisis and shaped those decisions.

**Misconception alert:** The existence of two alliance blocs did not mechanically start the war. The important issue is how alliances and strategic commitments helped turn the Austria-Serbia confrontation into a wider European crisis.

## 5. Rivalry and crises in the Balkans
The Balkans were sometimes described as the "powder keg of Europe" because nationalism, weakening Ottoman influence and competition among surrounding powers produced repeated tensions.

Austria-Hungary and Russia both had interests in the region. Serbia's ambitions and its connection to South Slav nationalism worried Austria-Hungary. The Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 changed the regional balance and strengthened Serbia, increasing Austrian concern.

This meant that by 1914 relations between Austria-Hungary and Serbia were already highly strained. Sarajevo did not create that rivalry; it activated it.

## 6. The Sarajevo assassination: the immediate trigger
On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo by **Gavrilo Princip**, a Bosnian Serb nationalist associated with the Young Bosnia movement. The assassination gave Austria-Hungary an opportunity to confront Serbia.

The killing should be described as the **immediate trigger**, not the complete explanation for the war. To understand why it became so destructive, we must follow the decisions made during the July Crisis.

## 7. The July Crisis: how the trigger became war
After the assassination, Austria-Hungary sought German backing. Germany gave strong support to its ally, often described by historians as the "blank cheque." Austria-Hungary then delivered a severe ultimatum to Serbia on 23 July 1914.

Serbia accepted many demands but not all of them. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July. Russia began military measures in support of Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. German forces invaded Belgium as part of Germany's western war plan; Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August.

The sequence demonstrates the interaction of the causes: Balkan nationalism produced the immediate dispute; alliances and strategic commitments widened it; military planning and mobilisation accelerated decisions; and longstanding rivalry made compromise more difficult.

# Put the causes together
A strong History answer does not treat the causes as unrelated bullets.

**Long-term pressures:** nationalism + imperial rivalry + militarism/arms competition + alliance relationships + Balkan instability.

**Immediate trigger:** assassination of Franz Ferdinand.

**Escalation mechanism:** July Crisis, ultimatums, mobilisation, alliance expectations and declarations of war.

The best explanation is therefore not "WWI happened because Franz Ferdinand was assassinated." It is that the assassination triggered a crisis in a European system already made dangerous by accumulated political and military tensions.

# Timeline to remember
- **1870–1871:** Franco-Prussian War; Germany emerges unified and France loses Alsace-Lorraine.
- **1882:** Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy.
- **1904:** Entente Cordiale between Britain and France.
- **1905–1906:** First Moroccan Crisis.
- **1907:** Anglo-Russian Convention helps complete the diplomatic alignment commonly called the Triple Entente.
- **1911:** Second Moroccan (Agadir) Crisis.
- **1912–1913:** Balkan Wars.
- **28 June 1914:** Franz Ferdinand assassinated at Sarajevo.
- **23 July 1914:** Austria-Hungary issues ultimatum to Serbia.
- **28 July 1914:** Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
- **1 August 1914:** Germany declares war on Russia.
- **3 August 1914:** Germany declares war on France.
- **4 August 1914:** Britain declares war on Germany after the German invasion of Belgium.

# Common misconceptions
### "The assassination was the only cause."
Incorrect. It was the immediate trigger of a crisis built on deeper tensions.

### "Alliances automatically forced every member to fight."
Too simplistic. Alliances and strategic commitments strongly influenced decisions and widened the crisis, but governments still made political choices.

### "Militarism simply means having weapons."
Incomplete. It includes the growing importance of military power, planning and assumptions in state policy as well as arms expansion.

### "Imperialism directly caused the Sarajevo crisis."
Not directly. Imperial rivalry contributed to the wider atmosphere of mistrust among European powers.

## Weak vs strong answer
**Weak:** "Militarism caused the war because countries had many weapons."

**Stronger:** "European powers expanded their armies and navies and developed plans for a possible war. This increased suspicion and made rapid mobilisation central to crisis decision-making. During the July Crisis, military preparations therefore helped turn a diplomatic confrontation into a wider military conflict."

The stronger answer earns its quality from the **historical mechanism**: it explains how the factor contributed to war.
