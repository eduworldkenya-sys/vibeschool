## Topic question
After two world wars, how did states try to manage conflict, cooperation, decolonisation and ideological rivalry without creating another global war?

## Why this topic follows World Wars
WWII destroyed the old balance of power, exposed the weakness of the League system, accelerated decolonisation, elevated the United States and Soviet Union, and created demand for stronger international cooperation. International Relations therefore continues the story rather than starting a disconnected topic.

# A. United Nations

## Formation
The UN grew out of wartime cooperation and plans for a post-war international organisation. The name `United Nations` was used during WWII for the Allied coalition, while later conferences developed institutional plans. Representatives of 50 countries met at San Francisco in 1945 to draft and sign the Charter; Poland, not represented at the conference, later signed and became one of the original 51 members. The UN officially came into existence on 24 October 1945 after the Charter entered into force.

### Why create another organisation after the League?
The League had shown that international institutions could support cooperation, but also that collective security failed when major powers were absent, divided or unwilling to act. The UN design therefore placed major-power cooperation at the centre of the Security Council while also creating broad institutions for political, economic, social, legal and humanitarian cooperation.

## Purposes
Learners should understand the purposes as connected ideas: maintaining international peace/security; developing friendly relations among nations; cooperating on economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems; promoting human rights; and providing a centre for harmonising international action.

## Organisation
### General Assembly
All UN member states are represented. It deliberates on matters within the Charter, approves the budget, elects members to various bodies and makes recommendations. On many important questions it does not operate like a world parliament passing binding domestic laws.

### Security Council
Has primary responsibility under the Charter for international peace and security. It has five permanent members—China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and United States—and elected non-permanent members. Permanent members possess the veto on substantive decisions. Explain both sides: the veto can prevent action when a permanent member objects, but the structure was designed partly around the reality that a security system without major-power participation might collapse like the League.

### Secretariat
Carries out day-to-day work under the Secretary-General and international staff. The Secretary-General also has diplomatic/political roles, but should not be described as a world president.

### International Court of Justice
Principal judicial organ, seated at The Hague. It settles legal disputes submitted by states and gives advisory opinions in authorised circumstances. It is different from the International Criminal Court.

### Economic and Social Council
Coordinates/reviews economic, social and related work across the UN system and provides a forum connecting states and specialised expertise/civil society under UN rules.

### Trusteeship Council
Created to supervise trust territories and support their advancement toward self-government/independence. It suspended regular operations after the last trust territory, Palau, became independent in 1994. This is a useful example of an organ whose historical mission largely concluded.

## Performance and challenges
### Peace and security
The UN has provided peacekeeping, mediation, sanctions and collective-security frameworks in many conflicts. Performance varies because the UN depends on member-state mandates, resources and political agreement. Peacekeeping itself developed in practice and is not simply a standing UN army.

### Decolonisation
The UN became an important forum and institutional environment for decolonisation, including trusteeship and General Assembly action. Do not claim the UN single-handedly caused independence; anti-colonial movements, imperial weakness and local/international politics were central.

### Human rights and humanitarian cooperation
The UN system helped develop international human-rights standards and coordinates humanitarian, refugee, health, development and child-related work through organs, programmes and specialised agencies. Distinguish the UN proper from autonomous specialised agencies while explaining the broader UN system.

### Challenges
Permanent-member veto and rivalry; dependence on member contributions and political will; difficulty enforcing decisions against powerful states; sovereignty constraints; unequal power; complex conflicts; funding/capacity problems; accusations of selectivity or slow response. Strong evaluation should balance institutional design with member-state behaviour.

## Misconceptions
- UN has its own permanent world army — false.
- General Assembly resolutions always bind every state like domestic statutes — false/overbroad.
- ICJ and ICC are the same — false.
- UN ended colonialism alone — false.

# B. Commonwealth

## Formation and evolution
The modern Commonwealth developed from the transformation of the British Empire as self-governing dominions and later independent states sought forms of voluntary association. The 1926 Imperial Conference/Balfour Declaration and Statute of Westminster 1931 were important in recognising dominion autonomy; the 1949 London Declaration enabled republican India to remain within the Commonwealth and is a major landmark in the modern association.

## Membership
Membership is voluntary and is not identical to former British colonial status: most members have historical British links, but the association has admitted states without such direct constitutional history. The British monarch is Head of the Commonwealth, a symbolic role distinct from being head of state of every member.

## Functions/areas of cooperation
Consultation among governments; support for democracy, rule of law and human rights commitments; technical/development cooperation; education/youth/cultural links; election observation and institutional support; Commonwealth Games as a visible sporting association. Avoid presenting every programme as equally important in every period.

## Challenges/performance
Diverse political/economic interests; limited enforcement powers; questions about relevance; human-rights/democratic controversies; unequal member capacities; voluntary cooperation. Yet shared language/institutions/networks and diplomatic access can facilitate cooperation. Evaluation should avoid both `Commonwealth is useless` and uncritical celebration.

# C. Non-Aligned Movement

## Background and formation
The Cold War divided much of international politics around US-led and Soviet-led blocs, while newly independent Asian and African states wanted greater autonomy in foreign policy and stronger opposition to colonialism. The 1955 Bandung Conference was an important precursor. The first NAM summit was held in Belgrade in 1961. Important leaders associated with early non-alignment included Josip Broz Tito, Jawaharlal Nehru, Gamal Abdel Nasser, Kwame Nkrumah and Sukarno, though their roles and the movement's institutional development should be source-verified in final claim ledger.

## Meaning of non-alignment
It did not necessarily mean political neutrality on every issue or equal distance from both superpowers at all times. It expressed an aspiration to preserve independent judgement, oppose colonialism/racial domination, reduce bloc domination and promote peaceful coexistence and development interests.

## Performance
NAM amplified the diplomatic voice of developing/newly independent states, supported anti-colonial causes, debated disarmament and peace, and promoted demands for fairer international economic relations. Its large membership gave political visibility but not uniform policy.

## Challenges
Members had different regimes, interests and security relationships; some leaned toward one superpower; interstate conflicts occurred among members; lack of strong enforcement machinery; post-Cold War questions about purpose. Teach the contradiction: a movement can be influential as a diplomatic coalition without behaving as a unified military/economic bloc.

# D. Cold War

## What was the Cold War?
A prolonged global rivalry after WWII centred on the United States and Soviet Union and their allies, involving ideological competition, military alliances, nuclear deterrence, propaganda, espionage, economic competition, arms/space races and proxy conflicts. `Cold` means the superpowers avoided sustained direct full-scale war against each other; it does not mean the period was peaceful—millions experienced violent wars elsewhere.

## Causes
### Ideological conflict
US-led liberal-capitalist and Soviet communist political-economic systems offered competing visions and distrusted each other's expansion.

### Wartime alliance breaks down
The US, Britain and USSR cooperated against Nazi Germany despite deep differences. Once the common enemy was defeated, disputes over post-war Europe became harder to contain.

### Eastern Europe
Soviet influence/control expanded across Eastern Europe, creating security depth for Moscow but appearing expansionist to Western governments. Each side interpreted the other's actions through fear and suspicion.

### Germany and post-war settlement
Occupation and reconstruction of Germany/Berlin became central disputes, eventually contributing to separate German states and repeated Berlin crises.

### Nuclear weapons/security dilemma
The atomic bomb and subsequent Soviet nuclear capability intensified strategic fear. Measures one side called defensive could appear offensive to the other, encouraging arms competition.

### Economic/political containment and rival blocs
The Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan and Western integration were interpreted by the Soviet leadership as hostile containment; Soviet consolidation and later institutions reinforced division. NATO (1949) and Warsaw Pact (1955) formalised military blocs.

## Europe case study
### Berlin Blockade and Airlift, 1948–49
The Soviet Union blocked Western land access to sectors of Berlin amid disputes over Germany/currency. Western powers supplied West Berlin by air. The blockade ended without direct superpower war. Significance: hardened division and demonstrated confrontation below the threshold of direct war.

### Berlin Wall, 1961
East German authorities, backed by the Soviet bloc, sealed the border in Berlin to stop large-scale movement from East Germany through West Berlin. The Wall became a powerful symbol of European division until 1989.

## Cuba case study — Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962
After the Cuban Revolution and deteriorating US-Cuban relations, Cuba aligned increasingly with the Soviet Union. The USSR secretly deployed nuclear missiles to Cuba. US reconnaissance detected the sites; President John F. Kennedy announced a naval `quarantine`. After tense negotiations, the Soviet Union agreed to remove missiles from Cuba; the US publicly pledged not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove US Jupiter missiles from Turkey within a separate understanding. Significance: the crisis brought the superpowers dangerously close to nuclear war and encouraged later crisis-management/arms-control measures.

## Vietnam case study
Vietnam's conflict grew from colonialism, Vietnamese nationalism, communist revolution and Cold War intervention. After French defeat and the 1954 Geneva settlement, Vietnam was temporarily divided, with rival governments in North and South. The United States escalated military involvement to prevent a communist victory; North Vietnam and the National Liberation Front fought South Vietnamese and US/allied forces, with Soviet/Chinese support to the North in varying forms. US combat forces withdrew after the 1973 Paris Peace Accords; Saigon fell to North Vietnamese forces in 1975 and Vietnam was reunified under communist rule.

**Significance:** illustrates containment, proxy/internationalised war, limits of superior military resources, domestic political costs and the danger of reducing a local/national conflict to superpower ideology alone.

## Angola case study
After Portuguese colonial rule collapsed, competing Angolan movements fought for power around independence in 1975. The MPLA received major Soviet and Cuban support; rival forces including UNITA received varying external backing, notably from the United States and apartheid South Africa at different stages. Angola became a major Cold War proxy arena intertwined with regional struggles and local political conflict. Teach local agency: Angolans were not merely puppets of Moscow/Washington.

## Course beyond case studies
Cold War tension moved through confrontation and periods of reduced tension (`détente`). Arms-control agreements and diplomacy coexisted with proxy wars. Renewed tension followed developments including the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan. In the 1980s, internal Soviet reform under Mikhail Gorbachev, economic/political pressures, changing Eastern European politics and diplomacy helped transform the rivalry. Communist governments in Eastern Europe fell in 1989; Germany reunified in 1990; the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991.

## Effects
- division of Europe and Germany for decades;
- NATO/Warsaw Pact and global alliance politics;
- nuclear arms race and deterrence/fear;
- proxy wars and enormous human costs outside the superpower homelands;
- military/technological and space competition;
- propaganda, espionage and domestic political repression in various states;
- foreign aid/development competition and intervention in newly independent states;
- influence on African conflicts and diplomacy;
- growth/relevance of non-alignment;
- after 1989–91, major transformation of European/global power relations.

## Historical thinking
`Was the Cold War caused more by ideology or security fears?` Require evidence from Eastern Europe, Germany and alliance/nuclear developments. A sophisticated answer may show how ideology shaped interpretation of genuine security concerns.

# Teacher layer

1. WWII result → UN formation.
2. Compare League and UN without claiming the UN simply `fixed` every League weakness.
3. Teach Commonwealth/NAM as different forms of international association.
4. Build Cold War cause web.

6. End with effects and comparison of international responses.

## Board summary
`WWII → UN` | `decolonisation → Commonwealth/NAM` | `US–USSR rivalry → blocs/nuclear competition/proxy wars` | `Europe + Cuba + Vietnam + Angola → Cold War course` | `1989–91 transformation`.

## Likely misconceptions
Cold War had no fighting; NAM members were neutral in every dispute; UN and League structures were identical; Commonwealth membership means British rule; every post-1945 conflict was caused solely by US–USSR rivalry.

# Student quick revision
UN: 1945, Charter, six principal organs, peace/security + cooperation, achievements constrained by member politics/veto/resources.
Commonwealth: evolved from empire into voluntary association; cooperation with limited coercive power.
NAM: Bandung precursor, Belgrade 1961, independent foreign-policy aspiration, anti-colonial/development voice, diverse membership.
Cold War: ideology + security + post-war Europe + nuclear rivalry; Europe/Cuba/Vietnam/Angola case studies; arms race/proxy wars/blocs; ends through 1989–91 transformation.
