## Chapter Four: The Funeral She Reached Too Late

Her father died in 2018.

The call came at 3:12 in the morning.

Mwende remembered the time because she had looked at the clock before answering and thought, *Who calls at this hour unless something terrible has happened?*

Peter was crying.

"Sister. Baba is gone."

For several seconds Mwende heard only the air conditioner above her head. Its steady hum seemed an insult. How could a machine continue doing an ordinary thing when her father had stopped breathing?

"What happened?"

"He was unwell last night. We took him to hospital."

"Which hospital?"

"He's gone, Mwende."

She sat on the edge of her narrow bed, her feet on the cold floor. On the wall above the wardrobe hung a calendar with a photograph of a desert at sunset.

"I spoke to him on Sunday."

"I know."

"He told me his chest felt better."

"I know."

"I told him I'd come in December."

Peter began crying again.

She requested leave. The travel arrangements were complicated by the timing and her work obligations. She did eventually get home, but after the burial. By the time she walked through her mother's gate, the chairs had been carried away and the cooking pots washed.

She stood in the compound wearing a black dress she had bought at the airport.

There was no crowd.

No procession.

No last chance to see her father's face.

Her mother came out and embraced her.

Mwende asked to be taken to the grave.

She knelt beside the fresh soil. Peter remained a short distance behind her. The small cross stood slightly crooked. She noticed that before she noticed the flowers, and the observation made her angry with herself.

She opened her mouth.

No words came.

She had imagined arriving home, holding her father's hand, telling him about the house and the land, telling him she would be around more often. Instead, she placed her palm against the earth.

"You told me to keep something for myself," she whispered. "I tried."

She stayed in Kenya for eighteen days.

During those days, she paid part of the hospital balance, helped cover expenses remaining from the funeral, bought new bedding for her mother, and arranged a monthly allowance.

It gave her something she knew how to do.

She could not undo an absence, but she could send money.

Before leaving, Peter asked if she wanted to move permanently into her house.

"Not yet," she said. "There are still things I need to finish."

"What things?"

She could not name them.

On the return flight to Riyadh, she cried beneath the airline blanket. Once she arrived, a household needed her. Someone asked about dinner. Someone had misplaced a scarf. Someone wanted a child taken to an appointment.

The requests were small and immediate. She understood all of them.

Grief was enormous and shapeless. Work arrived with a list.

She buried herself in the list.

Eight years later, her father's death still lived between her and the rest of the family. She knew they had not deliberately blamed her, but there were sentences that could still undo her.

*You weren't there.*

Nobody needed to say the words anymore. She heard them whenever she looked at the photograph taken at the grave, in which everyone stood close together and her space had not been left empty.

In 2026, on her second night back, she found her mother polishing the old tin cup.

"Baba used this for maize," Mwende said.

"I know."

"Why do you still keep it?"

Mama Kathini ran her thumb around the rim.

"Because when I hold it, I remember his hands."

Mwende sat beside her.

"Did he ask for me?"

Her mother kept polishing.

"He asked whether you were well."

"Did he ask when I was coming?"

"He knew you were working."

"That's not what I asked."

Mama Kathini stopped.

"Yes," she said. "He asked."

Mwende lowered her head.

Her mother put the cup down.

"Listen to me. You did not cause his death by working far away."

"I should have been here."

"Perhaps you should have visited more. Perhaps we should all have spoken differently about what mattered. But we cannot live backward."

Mwende began to cry.

Her mother did not comfort her with promises.

She sat beside her daughter until the crying slowed.

Then she said, "I don't want my old age paid for entirely in money, Mwende. Sometimes I want someone to sit here and tell me what she ate for breakfast."

That was the hardest thing her mother had ever asked of her.

## Chapter Five: Peter's Side of the Ledger

On the fifth morning, Mwende asked Peter for the rental records.

He arrived with a blue notebook, an envelope of receipts and his wife Ruth.

Mwende had not expected Ruth.

"Why is she here?"

"Because she knows what happens in this house," Peter said.

Ruth was quiet, observant and tired in a way that did not show until she sat down. She worked part-time at a small shop and had spent years helping Mama Kathini with hospital visits. She greeted Mwende respectfully and placed a thermos on the table.

"I thought we could talk properly," she said.

Mwende opened the notebook.

The entries were uneven. Some months showed rent paid on the third, others on the fifteenth. One tenant had delayed payment. Another had left damage in the bathroom. There were receipts for plumbing, painting, a gate repair, and medicine for their mother.

Mwende added figures on her phone.

"Peter. Why is the rent mixed with Mama's expenses?"

He shifted in his chair.

"Because that's where the money went."

"You didn't ask me."

"I sent messages."

"Messages saying Mama needed help. Not that you were using the rental money."

Peter looked away.

Ruth reached for a receipt.

"Sometimes we had to decide quickly," she said. "When Mama fell last year, the clinic wanted payment before some tests were done. Peter used what was available. He should have told you clearly afterward."

"I would have sent money."

"You were between contracts," Ruth replied.

Mwende stared at her.

Ruth continued, gently, "You had told us to manage with what was there until you settled again."

"I meant the household allowance."

"Yes. But we didn't agree what came from which account."

A silence filled the room.

Mwende noticed Peter's knuckles. They were rough and nicked from work. She suddenly remembered him in the unfinished house years earlier, carrying bags of cement while she directed the fundis on video.

"Did you take anything for yourself?" she asked.

Peter looked up sharply.

"For myself?"

"I need to know."

He laughed once, without humour.

"Do you think I've become rich from your house?"

"That's not what I said."

"Every person thinks I live on money from my sister abroad. Do you know how many times people have asked me to lend them something because 'Mwende can send'?"

Ruth rested her hand on his arm, but he continued.

"I took Mama to hospital. I dealt with tenants who refused to pay. I chased fundis. I went to check the Syokimau fence because you were afraid somebody would move the beacons. I did those things. Sometimes I used the rent and sometimes I used my own money. But I did them."

Mwende closed the notebook.

"Why didn't you tell me you were struggling?"

Peter stared.

"Because every time you called, you asked for progress. Is the house okay? Is Mama okay? Is the land okay? You didn't ask how I was."

The words struck her more deeply than an accusation would have.

"I paid you."

"Sometimes. And I'm grateful. But you were my sister before you became my employer."

Ruth looked at the empty chairs.

"I think both of you have been tired for years," she said.

Mwende turned toward her.

"What do you mean?"

"You send money and worry whether people are wasting it. Peter handles problems and worries you'll think he is stealing. Neither of you trusts the other to admit things are hard."

Peter stared at the floor.

Mwende began sorting the receipts into piles.

They spent the morning separating rental income from repairs, family contributions and Peter's own expenses. It was imperfect. A few months could not be fully reconstructed. Some money had been spent without records; some bills had been paid twice by mistake. Peter agreed to provide clearer statements going forward. Mwende agreed to set aside separate funds for the property and her mother's support, so neither would be taken from the other without discussion.

By late afternoon, the table was covered in paper.

Ruth stood to leave.

At the door, she turned back.

"Mwende, we are not asking you to stop helping. We are asking you to let us be family without every conversation becoming an account."

Mwende wanted to answer.

Instead, she looked at the receipts spread across the table.

For seventeen years, sending money had felt like love made visible.

She had not considered how often love could begin to feel like a debt.

## Chapter Six: The Child Who Knew Her as a Screen

Peter's daughter Wema was twelve.

She had been born while Mwende was abroad. When she was small, Mwende had watched her first steps on a grainy video. She had seen her lose a tooth through a phone camera. She had paid for school shoes and birthday dresses and a bicycle that Ruth had warned was too large.

Wema called her *Auntie Saudi*.

Mwende did not like the name, but she had never asked the child to stop.

On Saturday, Wema came to visit with a small backpack and a book she was reading for school.

"Auntie, you said you'd bring me headphones."

Mwende opened her suitcase.

"They're here."

Wema smiled, took the box and examined it.

"Thank you."

Then she sat beside the window and began testing them.

Mwende watched her. The girl had Peter's eyebrows and Ruth's seriousness. She looked older than the last video Mwende remembered.

"What do you like doing now?" Mwende asked.

Wema looked up.

"Reading."

"What kind of books?"

"Stories."

"What stories?"

The girl gave a title.

Mwende did not recognise it.

"Tell me about it."

Wema studied her aunt with mild suspicion.

"Do you really want to know?"

"Of course."

"You usually ask about my grades."

Mwende felt her face grow warm.

"I ask because school matters."

"I know."

Wema removed the headphones.

"When we talk on video, you ask whether I've eaten, whether I'm studying and whether I need anything."

"Those are good questions."

"Yes. But you never ask what made me laugh that day."

Mwende leaned back.

The child went on, not cruelly, simply stating something she had noticed.

"I know you're good, Auntie. Mum says you've helped us a lot. But I don't know what you like. I don't know what you do when you're not working."

Mwende tried to answer.

She liked tidy rooms. She liked strong tea. She liked the early morning before the rest of a household woke.

Those were not quite hobbies.

"I used to like singing," she said finally.

Wema looked surprised.

"You sing?"

"When I was younger."

"Which songs?"

Mwende laughed. "Old ones."

"Sing one."

"No."

"Please."

"I've forgotten."

"Then we can learn one together."

There was such confidence in the offer that Mwende had to look away.

That afternoon, they cooked together. Mwende showed Wema how to make a simple rice dish with spices she had learned to use in Riyadh. Wema chopped vegetables slowly, asking why this went in before that, why the rice needed soaking, why Mwende measured salt in her palm rather than with a spoon.

For once, Mwende was not cooking to meet an instruction.

She was cooking because somebody wanted to learn from her.

Later they sat outside, eating from two plates balanced on their knees.

Wema told her about a school debate she had lost, a friend who talked too much and a teacher who always said, "Read the question twice."

Mwende listened.

As the light faded, Wema rested her head briefly against Mwende's shoulder.

It was a small thing.

No speech.

No promise.

But Mwende felt the weight of it all the way down her arm.

When Ruth came to collect her daughter, Wema asked, "Auntie, are you leaving soon?"

Mwende hesitated.

"I have a ticket."

"So yes?"

"I don't know."

Wema nodded, trying to appear older than she was.

"Okay."

Then she said, "Next time, can we do something that isn't shopping?"

Mwende smiled.

"Yes."

"Promise?"

She wanted to say yes quickly, the way she had promised many things from a distance.

Instead she said, "I'll do my best to keep that promise. And if something changes, I'll tell you."

Wema accepted that.

After they left, Mwende carried the plates to the kitchen.

She washed them slowly, thinking of a niece who had known her first as a voice, then as a face on a screen, then as the person who paid for things.

It would take time to become an aunt in the fuller sense of the word.

For the first time, she wanted to spend that time.
