## Chapter Seven: In Someone Else's Kitchen

Mwende's body still woke at 4:45 a.m.

Sometimes she lay in bed wondering what she had forgotten. Sometimes she rose and went straight to the kitchen, opening cupboards before remembering she was not responsible for anybody's breakfast.

In her earlier years abroad, she had worked for an employer who counted everything: eggs, detergent, phone calls, minutes spent sitting. When Mwende had once asked to call home about her father's illness, the woman had said it could wait until the floor was finished.

Mwende had finished the floor.

She had never forgotten the shame of it, nor the anger that came afterward when she imagined what she should have said.

Later she worked for another family. Madam Aisha was not perfect. She could become demanding when relatives visited, and sometimes she changed plans without warning. But she listened when Mwende said she needed a day to rest. They had discussed her duties, her pay and time off. When the household's grandmother was ill, Mwende helped because she cared for her, not merely because she was ordered to.

By the time Mwende had worked in that house for several years, she knew the family as intimately as some relatives in Kenya.

She knew that Aisha's daughter Salma hated raisins in rice.

She knew that the grandmother grew quiet when she was anxious and asked twice for the same glass of water.

She knew the youngest boy was frightened of thunderstorms, although he insisted he was not.

She knew the sound of the front gate, the way the kettle clicked before boiling and the exact position of the small blue medicine bags.

There had been affection there.

There had also been a wage.

The difference mattered.

One afternoon in Kenya, Madam Aisha called.

"Mwende, how is your mother?"

"She is well today."

"Good. Take care of her. She waited a long time for you."

Mwende smiled.

Then Aisha said, "Salma has left for university. The house is very quiet."

"Already?"

"She is eighteen. Children grow even when we are not ready."

Mwende sat down.

She had first met Salma when the girl was small enough to hide behind the sofa.

"She used to ask me for stories," Mwende said.

"She still remembers them."

Aisha paused.

"Have you decided when to return?"

Mwende looked toward the kitchen wall.

Her ticket was booked. She had not told Aisha she was uncertain.

"I am thinking."

"Take the time you agreed on. If you need to discuss another arrangement, tell me honestly. But I must also plan for the house."

Mwende nodded.

The honesty unsettled her.

In her mind, she had believed the household could not manage without her. Now she heard the truth: they valued her, but their lives would continue. They would arrange help. Salma would keep growing. Aisha would still be Aisha.

Mwende was not the centre of their world.

That should have hurt.

Instead it loosened something inside her.

"Madam," she asked, "when you are alone, what do you do?"

Aisha laughed. "Alone? I drink tea. I call my sister. Sometimes I sit and do nothing."

"Doesn't it feel strange?"

"At first. Then it feels like life."

After the call, Mwende remained at the table.

She had been working so long that she sometimes measured her worth by the number of things she could do before anyone else noticed they needed doing.

She wondered who she would be if nobody asked her to do any of them.

The question frightened her.

But for the first time, it also interested her.

## Chapter Eight: Faith Counts the Cost of Tea

Faith lived in a modest rented house outside Nairobi with two children, a refrigerator that made loud noises and a catering business that operated from her kitchen.

She had returned from Saudi Arabia permanently three years earlier.

When Mwende arrived, Faith was standing over a pot of beans with a pencil tucked behind her ear.

"Come in. Don't admire the house; admire the food."

Mwende laughed.

The kitchen smelled of onions and freshly chopped coriander. Containers were stacked on a metal shelf. A handwritten list was taped to the wall:

**RICE — BEANS — CHAPATI — CUSTOMER CALLS — SCHOOL PICKUP**

Faith looked tired.

But not lost.

"You must be making good money," Mwende said.

Faith burst out laughing.

"That's what people say when they see you working."

She spread a notebook on the table. On one page were customer payments. On another were costs: vegetables, cooking gas, packaging, transport, helpers on busy days, broken utensils, wasted food.

"Last month I sold a lot," Faith said. "Do you know how much I kept?"

Mwende guessed.

Faith shook her head.

"Less than you'd think. Some days I work all morning and the profit is smaller than what I used to earn for one day's work abroad."

"Then why continue?"

"Because the business is improving. Because my children are here. Because I decided to try something I could build beside them. Not because it's easier."

Mwende touched the notebook.

"I don't know how to run a business."

"You know how to manage food."

"That's different."

"Of course it is. You would have to learn prices, customers and suppliers. But don't pretend you've learned nothing in seventeen years."

Mwende looked at her.

Faith continued.

"You've organised households. Tracked purchases. Managed time. Planned meals for visitors. Learned a language. Worked for people with different expectations. Those are skills."

"Not the kind people pay for here."

"Maybe some people would. Maybe not enough to live on immediately. We would have to find out."

Faith handed her a chopping board.

"For today, cut tomatoes."

Mwende started working. Her hands knew what to do.

After an hour she asked to see how orders were priced. Faith showed her how she calculated ingredients, labour and delivery. They disagreed about the quantity of oil needed for a large order. Faith argued from her customer experience; Mwende argued from seventeen years of cooking.

Neither gave in easily.

By the end of the morning, they were both laughing.

When a customer cancelled two trays of food without warning, Faith went quiet.

"What now?" Mwende asked.

"I try selling the portions locally. Some may be wasted. This is what I mean about business."

Mwende looked at the food.

"What if we package smaller portions?"

Faith considered.

"That might work."

They did. Some sold; some did not. The day's accounts showed a modest profit, not a miracle.

They sat beneath a small awning afterward, drinking tea from chipped cups.

"Sometimes I miss Saudi," Faith admitted.

Mwende looked surprised.

"Even now?"

"Yes. I miss knowing what day the salary would arrive. I miss some of the people. I don't miss being away from my children. Both things can be true."

Mwende stared into her cup.

"I think people expect me to hate it there."

"Do you?"

"No."

"Then don't pretend. But don't pretend you love every part of it either."

Faith looked at her.

"Tell me something, Mwende. If all your property were sold tomorrow and the money placed in your account, would you know how you wanted to spend your days?"

Mwende opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

Faith nodded.

"That's the work you have to do. Not building another house."

On the way home, Mwende stopped at a shop and bought a small exercise book.

On its first page she wrote:

**THINGS I KNOW HOW TO DO.**

She stared at the line for several minutes.

Then began to write.

## Chapter Nine: The Lunch Nobody Enjoyed

The family lunch was meant to celebrate Mwende's return.

Mama Kathini cooked pilau and vegetables. Peter bought soda. Ruth brought chapati. Wema laid out the cups and insisted that everyone use the matching ones because "Auntie is a guest."

Mwende almost corrected her.

Instead, she smiled and helped set the table.

Relatives arrived with embraces and questions.

"How is Saudi?"

"How much are domestic workers paid now?"

"Can you get a job for my sister's daughter?"

"Do you think you can help with the church roof?"

One aunt admired Mwende's shoes and asked their price.

Another said, "At least you are comfortable. Some of us stayed in Kenya and have nothing."

Mwende looked down at the food.

A cousin who had struggled to keep his small shop open asked whether she could lend him money. He had a plan, he said. A very good plan. He only needed capital.

Mwende asked for details.

He grew annoyed.

"Why must you ask so many questions? You're my cousin."

"Because I need to understand how you'll repay it."

The room became uncomfortable.

Peter tried to make a joke.

"Our Mwende has become a banker."

Everyone laughed except Ruth.

Aunties began discussing marriage. One suggested that Mwende had waited too long. Another said she should find a respectable man to look after the houses.

Mwende lifted her head.

"I can look after my houses."

The aunt laughed.

"Even now you talk like somebody working abroad. In Kenya you need people."

"I have people."

"Then why do you keep leaving them?"

No one answered.

Mama Kathini placed a serving spoon on the table.

"Let's eat."

But the silence had already changed.

Then the cousin returned to his request.

"You can build in Syokimau but can't help me with a small amount?"

Mwende set down her fork.

The words escaped before she had decided to say them.

"Why does everybody think my money is waiting in a bag?"

He looked offended.

"Nobody said that."

"You don't have to say it. Every time I come, someone has a problem that begins with my salary and ends with my responsibility."

Peter frowned.

"Sister, don't speak as if the whole family depends on you."

"Then stop making me feel as if I owe everybody an explanation for every shilling I spend."

The cousin pushed his chair back.

"So now you're too important for us."

Mwende laughed bitterly.

"I work in somebody else's house, and you think I'm too important?"

Mama Kathini stood.

"Enough."

Her voice was not loud, but everyone heard it.

"Nobody will insult my daughter at my table. And nobody will shame people who have asked for help. We can say no without humiliating each other."

Mwende covered her mouth.

Her mother turned to her.

"And you, my child, have been angry for years. But you cannot come home only to punish people for the questions you were too tired to answer."

The words were sharp because they were true.

Mwende stood and walked into the compound.

She sat on a low wall near the avocado tree.

After a while Ruth came outside.

For several minutes they listened to children playing beyond the fence.

"I made everything worse," Mwende said.

"You said things people needed to hear," Ruth replied. "And some things you need to say again, more calmly."

Mwende wiped her face.

"They don't understand how hard I worked."

"Some don't. Some do. Mama knows. Peter knows more than you think."

"Then why do they laugh when I ask about money?"

"Because people get embarrassed when they feel accused. You get frightened when they ask. Everyone reaches for an old habit."

Ruth sat beside her.

"When my daughter was born, Peter called you. You paid part of the hospital bill. Do you know what he said afterward?"

Mwende shook her head.

"He said, 'My sister has saved us.' He was proud of you. But he was also ashamed he couldn't manage it himself."

Mwende stared at the ground.

"Why didn't he tell me?"

"Because in this family we praise sacrifice more easily than we admit fear."

They sat quietly.

Inside, someone began collecting dishes.

After a while Wema appeared carrying a piece of cake.

"Auntie?"

"Yes?"

"Do you want this?"

Mwende took the plate.

"Thank you."

Wema sat on the wall between the two women.

"Are you and Uncle Peter fighting?"

"Not forever," Mwende said.

"Good. Because he promised to take us to the market tomorrow."

Mwende laughed through her tears.

And for a moment, the family drama reduced itself to something small enough to hold: a piece of cake, a child, an ordinary plan for tomorrow.
