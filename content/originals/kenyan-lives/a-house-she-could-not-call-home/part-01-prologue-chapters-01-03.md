# A HOUSE SHE COULD NOT CALL HOME

**A VibeSchool Original · Kenyan family drama · Complete text-only edition**

*She had spent seventeen years building a life she could afford. Now she had to learn whether she could live inside it.*

---

## Prologue: The Woman at Arrivals

The first message arrived before Mwende's suitcase did.

**Madam Aisha:** *Where are the small blue medicine bags?*

The plane from Saudi Arabia had landed at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport an hour earlier. Mwende stood beside the baggage carousel, tired from the night flight, with the damp collar of her sweater folded against her throat. A man at the far end of the hall was shouting into his phone. A little girl was asleep on top of a trolley. Everywhere, people were being welcomed, claimed, hurried away.

Mwende typed without thinking.

*Second drawer on the left, beneath the white towels. The one marked Salma.*

Three dots appeared.

*Shukran, Mwende. We miss you.*

She smiled before she realised she was smiling.

Her Kenyan SIM card began to receive messages.

**Peter:** *Sister, fuel has gone up. Send something for the airport trip.*

**Caretaker:** *The kitchen tap is leaking again.*

**Church family group:** *Welcome home, our daughter. Remember the fundraising on Sunday.*

**Mama:** *You have landed? Please call me.*

Mwende had been away and back since 2009. She owned a three-bedroom house in Machakos County, another plot of land, and a residential plot in Syokimau. She could speak enough Arabic to calm an angry employer, prepare six dishes for visitors she had never met, and remember which medicine belonged to which grandmother in a household of twelve.

Yet standing at the airport, she had no idea what she was going to do on Tuesday.

She called her mother.

"Ni mimi, Mama. I have arrived."

"Thank God," the old woman said. "Come slowly. The roads are not running away."

"I'm coming."

"Have you eaten?"

"On the plane."

"That is not food. I have cooked."

Her mother paused.

"How long are you staying this time, Mwende?"

The question came so gently that it hurt.

"I don't know yet."

The old woman made a small sound, not quite a sigh.

"Come first."

Mwende slipped the phone into her bag. Behind her, the carousel began moving. A suitcase that looked just like hers came around twice without anybody claiming it.

She watched it with an unreasonable ache.

There were things you could own and still fail to recognise as yours.

## Chapter One: The Key and the Gate

Peter arrived forty minutes late in a silver car that belonged to his friend.

He climbed out smiling, arms wide. He was her younger brother by three years, although people had begun mistaking him for the older one. There was grey in his beard and a softness around his middle that had not been there when she left Kenya the first time.

"My Saudi sister!"

"Don't call me that."

"Then stop coming from Saudi."

He hugged her hard. Mwende felt how thin his shoulders were under the shirt.

On the road to Syokimau, Peter talked about new apartments and a bypass, about the price of cement and a councillor who had promised to repair a drainage ditch. He pointed at every new development as though he had built it himself.

"Your land here is gold now," he told her. "Just wait. One day you'll see."

Mwende looked through the window. Her residential plot stood behind a wire fence in a neighbourhood of houses at different stages of hope: one painted cream, one still bare block, one abandoned at the first-floor slab. Grass pushed through the stones beside her gate. An aircraft passed low overhead.

"Has anybody checked the beacons?" she asked.

"Of course."

"When?"

Peter moved his hand on the steering wheel. "Recently."

"How recently?"

"Sister, you haven't even had tea."

It was always too early to ask questions until it was suddenly too late to ask them.

Her Machakos house was an hour or more away, depending on traffic. She had built it in stages: foundation after a good contract, walls after another, roof when she could finally afford it. The house had three bedrooms and a water tank. The sitting room had large windows because she had told the fundi she wanted air and light when she came home.

She had spent more time deciding the colour of those windows over WhatsApp than she had spent sitting beneath them.

A tenant had moved out a week earlier. Peter turned the key at the gate, but the lock resisted. He pushed; the metal scraped. The courtyard smelled of dust and wet leaves. A fallen branch lay beneath the mango tree.

"Needs a little cleaning," he said.

Inside, the floor echoed. On one wall a pale rectangle marked where the tenant had hung a television. A nail remained near the kitchen door. The bedroom she thought of as *hers* had a water stain beneath the window.

Mwende carried her suitcase inside and stood without moving.

Peter opened the curtains.

"See? Your own house. Nobody can tell you what to do here."

She nodded. There was no sofa, only two plastic chairs the caretaker had left. The kitchen cupboards were empty. She could not remember where the light switches were.

"Do you have a blanket?" she asked.

"I thought you were staying with Mama first."

"I wanted to see the house."

"Then we can go to Mama's."

She looked around at the rooms she had paid for. Through the window, the mango tree moved in the wind.

"Who has been sleeping here?" she asked.

"Nobody."

"Then why does it smell of other people's soap?"

Peter did not know how to answer.

They drove to their mother's place before sunset.

At the gate, Mama Kathini came toward them with both hands raised. She was smaller than Mwende remembered. Her skirt hung loosely at the waist, and she walked with the careful steps of a woman who had learned to negotiate pain before every movement.

"Child," she said, holding Mwende's face. "Let me look at you."

Mwende bent and embraced her.

For a while they said nothing. Then her mother drew back.

"You have become a visitor who brings her own towel."

Mwende laughed, but the words lodged somewhere behind her ribs.

That night she slept in the room where she had grown up. The curtain was new. The old wardrobe remained. On the top shelf stood a tin cup her father had used to measure maize before he died.

At midnight she woke, reaching for a phone that had not rung.

She lay still, waiting for someone to call her name.

No one did.

The quiet grew until she could hear the neighbour's radio through the wall.

In Riyadh, every sound after midnight had meant something: a child awake, a visitor arriving, the kettle needing water, Madam asking where a scarf had been placed. Here, silence offered no instructions.

She switched on the light and began folding clothes she had already folded.

## Chapter Two: The Girl Who Left in 2009

In 2009, when Mwende was twenty-five, her father walked her to the matatu stage carrying her suitcase by its broken handle.

He was still strong then, with a habit of clearing his throat before saying anything important. Her mother had packed two packets of groundnuts and a little container of dry chapati for the journey to Nairobi.

"Work hard," her father said.

"Yes, Baba."

"Don't believe every promise you hear."

"Yes."

"And don't send every coin you earn. Keep some for yourself."

Her mother laughed at him. "You make it sound as if she's leaving for a war."

He looked at the suitcase.

"She is leaving for a country where we don't know anybody. Let her remember she can call us."

Mwende had found the opportunity through a recruitment contact. The promises were simple: domestic work, accommodation, meals, a regular salary. She had been selling second-hand clothes without enough capital to replace the stock. Some weeks she earned something; other weeks she borrowed to pay back what she owed. The uncertainty was swallowing her.

Her first month in Saudi Arabia lasted longer than any month she had known.

The house was large and bright, with cool floors and a kitchen filled with machines she had never used. Her Arabic was almost nonexistent. She understood gestures, anger and the word for water before she understood instructions.

The family employed several people, but work did not fall equally between them. There were days when she finished late and mornings when she began before her body felt rested. When she made mistakes, she sometimes heard her name spoken sharply from another room.

She called her mother from the laundry area at night, lowering her voice.

"Are you eating?" Mama Kathini asked.

"Yes."

"Are they treating you well?"

Mwende looked at a basket of clothes that still had to be ironed.

"I'm learning."

The answer was not a lie. It was not the whole truth either.

Some households were harsh. Others, later, treated her with considerable kindness. In one, a woman taught her to cook kabsa slowly, explaining every step. In another, Mwende learned that finishing the assigned work did not always mean she was free to rest. She gradually learned which boundaries she could insist on, which contracts were worth renewing, and how to recognise promises she should ask to see written down.

On payday, the numbers on her phone made her breathe easier.

She sent money home.

Her mother replaced the leaking roof.

Peter paid for a training course.

Her father bought medicine without asking the neighbour to lend him cash.

Every transfer seemed to turn discomfort into something useful.

Then her father called one evening.

"My daughter, have you kept anything?"

"A little."

"Keep more."

"Mama needs the money."

"We need you more than we need money."

Mwende was standing in a storeroom when he said it. She looked at the neat rows of folded sheets, the extra blankets, the expensive luggage stacked for people who could leave whenever they wanted.

"I'll come home soon," she said.

She believed it.

She did not yet understand how easily *soon* could become a way of postponing a life.

## Chapter Three: A Roof Bought One Month at a Time

When Mwende bought her first plot, the news travelled through the family before she had finished her shift.

Peter called shouting, "Sister! You are now a landowner!"

Her mother ululated over the phone. Cousins asked what she planned to build. Neighbours congratulated Mama Kathini as though the land had already risen from the earth into a house.

Mwende said she would take it slowly.

She sent money for the foundation. Peter photographed the trenches. A mason sent measurements. A relative knew a cheaper supplier; another warned that supplier cheated people. Mwende received competing advice at four in the morning, Saudi time, between preparing breakfast and sorting uniforms.

The house grew in fragments on her phone.

One month, columns.

Another month, walls.

Then iron sheets.

Then doors wrapped in plastic.

Once, when the money for windows was short, she delayed buying herself a winter coat. She wrapped a second sweater beneath her work clothes and told herself the cold would pass.

When the roof finally went on, Peter sent a video. He stood in the empty sitting room, panning slowly toward the bedrooms.

"Here is your palace!"

Mwende played it seven times.

The family gathered at the site one Sunday to pray. Her father was in the photographs, standing near the gate, one hand resting on the stone wall. He had lost weight, but his smile was broad.

They placed her phone on a plastic chair and called her on video.

"How do you feel?" the pastor asked.

"Happy," Mwende said.

Everyone clapped.

Later that night, she returned to the kitchen and washed a stack of serving dishes.

She watched soap run into the sink and imagined the unfinished kitchen in her house.

*One day,* she told herself, *I'll wash my own dishes in my own kitchen and nobody will be waiting behind me with another task.*

When she came home during one of her breaks, the house was complete enough to stay in. The walls were cream. The windows were wide. She made tea and sat on a plastic chair, listening to children playing beyond the gate.

By noon, she had swept every room.

By two, she had washed the windows.

By four, she had rearranged the kitchen shelves.

At sunset she sat down again, unable to shake the feeling that she had forgotten something important.

She had built the house by giving every month a purpose.

Now that it existed, she did not know what purpose to give an ordinary afternoon.

So she found a tenant.

At first she called that a sensible investment.

Later she wondered whether it had also been an escape.

The rent began coming in. Most of it was spent on repairs, family needs and emergencies she could never quite predict. Mwende kept working. A second plot followed. Much later, a residential plot in Syokimau.

People praised her discipline.

Nobody saw the fear beneath it: if she stopped earning, she might discover that the buildings were all she had learned to make.
