## Chapter Ten: A Plot in Syokimau

Three days before her return flight, Mwende went to Syokimau alone.

She took a matatu and then walked along a dusty road between new apartment blocks. The sun was high. A builder called out to men unloading sand. A woman beside a kiosk asked whether she was looking for someone.

"No," Mwende replied. "I'm coming to see my land."

The woman glanced toward the fenced plots.

"Which one?"

Mwende pointed.

"That one."

The woman nodded. "The one with the crooked post."

Mwende stopped.

"Crooked?"

"That corner post. It has been leaning for months."

Mwende walked to the fence. The post leaned toward the road, its wire still attached. She knelt to look at the concrete base. Grass and red soil had gathered around it. A bottle lay beneath a thorn bush.

This was the land she had imagined as proof she could come home.

She had collected photographs of beautiful houses for years. Some had balconies that overlooked gardens. Some had enormous kitchens. She had saved pictures of cream tiles, wooden doors and a dining table large enough for everybody.

Now she stood before an empty plot and felt none of the excitement those images used to bring.

A man from a neighbouring house came out carrying a watering can.

"Are you the owner?"

"Yes."

"I've seen your brother here."

"Peter."

"He checks sometimes."

Mwende nodded.

The neighbour pointed at a ditch near the road.

"During heavy rain, water gathers along this side. When you build, make sure you get proper advice on drainage."

"Thank you."

She stood looking at the ground.

Someone needed to repair the fence. Someone needed to pay the land charges when due. If she built, there would be drawings, approvals, engineers, contractors, suppliers, payments, disputes, delays.

And afterward, perhaps, a second house she would not know how to inhabit.

She took out her phone and scrolled through the images she had collected.

One showed an enormous cream house with a staircase curving toward a chandelier.

She had once sent that picture to Peter with the words: *One day.*

Now she deleted it.

Not because she had stopped wanting beauty.

Because she was no longer certain that bigger rooms would solve the problem of an empty life.

She called Peter.

"Can you meet me here?"

He sounded worried.

"Is something wrong?"

"The corner post is leaning."

"Ah. That one."

"You knew?"

"Yes. I was going to fix it."

"When?"

He exhaled. "I don't have the money right now."

Mwende closed her eyes.

"Then why didn't you tell me?"

"Because when I tell you about a problem, you think I'm asking you for money."

She leaned against the gate.

He was right.

"Come anyway," she said. "We need to talk."

He arrived half an hour later on a boda boda.

They walked the boundary together, checking the visible beacons without disturbing them. Peter pointed out the neighbouring developments. Mwende showed him the drainage issue.

Then they sat on two stones beside the road.

"I thought this land would make me happy," she said.

Peter looked at her.

"It still can."

"Maybe. But I keep buying places to live in the future."

"Is that bad?"

"No. I just haven't made a life in the present."

Peter sat quietly.

"Do you know what I thought when you first left?" he asked.

"What?"

"That one day you would come home and everything would be easy."

Mwende smiled sadly.

"Me too."

He picked up a small stone and turned it between his fingers.

"But it wasn't easy for you there. And it hasn't been easy here."

Mwende looked at him.

"I'm frightened of becoming dependent on you."

Peter laughed in surprise.

"On me?"

"Yes. Of coming home, not earning, then needing you to take care of everything."

His smile faded.

"I thought you didn't trust me."

"I don't always. But it's more than that. I don't trust myself to live without a salary."

Peter nodded slowly.

"I understand the salary part. Sometimes I lie awake worrying about next month too."

"Then why do you make jokes when I ask where the money goes?"

"Because it makes me feel small."

They sat watching a lorry manoeuvre slowly along the rough road.

After a while Mwende said, "We need clear arrangements. Not because you're dishonest. Because we're both tired of guessing."

Peter nodded.

"And I need to stop promising to do things I can't afford."

"Yes."

He laughed.

"You said that very quickly."

"Because it's true."

She pointed at the corner post.

"Let's start with the fence. A proper quotation. Then a budget. The big house can wait."

Peter looked toward the empty land.

"You've changed."

Mwende considered.

"Not enough yet."

## Chapter Eleven: The Accounts of a Life

At Faith's suggestion, Mwende sat down with a notebook and listed everything she owned.

The house in Machakos County.

The separate plot.

The residential plot in Syokimau.

The money in her accounts.

The things she had promised her mother.

The property expenses that would continue whether she was in Kenya or Saudi Arabia.

Then she made a second list.

Not assets.

Expenses.

Food. Transport. Healthcare. Repairs. Utilities. Insurance if she could afford suitable cover. Contributions she chose to make rather than contributions others assumed she would make. Money for emergencies.

For years she had answered the question *Am I successful?* by thinking of how much property she had.

She had rarely answered the question *Could I support myself if my salary stopped?*

The answer was uncomfortable.

The house earned rent when occupied, but tenants could leave. Repairs were unpredictable. The two plots produced no reliable income. She had some savings, but much less than relatives assumed, because she had repeatedly put money into land and construction.

She was not poor.

She was not as financially secure as people imagined.

And she was not foolish for being afraid.

Still, the figures told her something useful.

She could make choices without pretending every choice would work.

She met with Peter and Ruth again. This time nobody raised their voice.

They agreed that Peter would no longer handle every property issue informally. He could continue helping, but responsibilities, payments and records would be clear. Repairs beyond an agreed amount would be discussed first, except in genuine emergencies. Mama Kathini's support would be treated as a separate family commitment, not silently taken from the rent.

Ruth offered to help organise the receipts.

Mwende shook her head.

"You already do too much."

Ruth smiled.

"Then you can learn to do them with me."

They spent an afternoon entering dates and amounts into a simple spreadsheet.

Wema watched from the sofa, curious.

"Auntie, are you doing homework?"

Mwende laughed.

"Something like that."

"What subject?"

"Learning how not to be confused by my own money."

Wema considered.

"Sounds difficult."

"It is."

"Can I help?"

Mwende gave her a stack of receipts to arrange by date. Wema worked slowly, asking questions, occasionally discovering that a bill had been placed in the wrong month.

By evening, they had made progress.

There was no miracle figure at the bottom of the page.

No sudden discovery that Mwende was secretly wealthy.

Only a clearer understanding of what she had and what she needed.

That night she called Faith.

"I want to learn how you cost your orders," she said.

"Good."

"And I want to understand bookkeeping properly."

"Also good."

"But I'm not opening a business tomorrow."

Faith laughed.

"Even better."

Mwende hesitated.

"I may still need to return to Saudi."

"Then go if you choose to. Just don't go because you're too afraid to stay for a single ordinary day."

After the call, Mwende opened a drawer.

Inside lay the keys to her Machakos house.

She took them out and placed them beside her notebook.

Three properties.

Several accounts.

A family that loved her and sometimes exhausted her.

A life she had postponed for seventeen years.

She wrote one question beneath the figures.

**What do I want an ordinary Wednesday to look like?**

For a long time she couldn't answer.

Then she wrote:

*Breakfast with Mama.*

*A walk.*

*Work that belongs to me, even if it is small.*

*Someone to laugh with in the evening.*

She stared at the list.

It looked embarrassingly modest beside the plans she had once made for a grand house in Syokimau.

But it was the first plan in years that included her actually being present.

## Chapter Twelve: The Flight She Did Not Take

The departure date arrived on a Tuesday.

Mwende woke before dawn.

Her suitcase stood beside the bedroom door.

The clothes inside were folded neatly. Her passport lay in a pouch. Her return documents were ready. She had packed without deciding.

She made tea.

At six, Peter called.

"Are we going to the airport?"

"I haven't decided."

There was a pause.

"Sister, the flight won't wait while you think."

"I know."

He softened.

"Do you want me to come?"

"Not yet."

She called Madam Aisha.

It was afternoon in Riyadh.

"Hello, Mwende."

"Madam. I need to talk about the contract."

Aisha listened while Mwende explained. She spoke of her mother's age, the uncertainty of being home, the savings she needed to organise and the pressure she felt to return before she had made any decision.

Aisha was quiet for a moment.

"I understand why you want more time," she said.

"I don't want to disappoint you."

"Then tell me honestly what you can do. That is better than promising something and regretting it."

Mwende looked at the packed suitcase.

"Can we discuss extending my leave?"

"We can discuss it. I can't promise the position will remain open indefinitely. I have responsibilities too."

"I understand."

"And Mwende?"

"Yes?"

"You have been good to this family. You do not have to prove it by coming back before you're ready."

After the call, Mwende sat still.

She had expected anger.

She had expected pleading.

She had expected to hear that she was indispensable.

Instead she had been treated like a person making a decision.

The freedom frightened her more than pressure would have.

She called her mother next.

"Mama."

"Yes?"

"I might stay longer."

The old woman did not shout or praise God.

"How much longer?"

"I don't know yet. I need to organise things."

"All right."

"Are you happy?"

"Of course. But I don't want you to stay because you think I will die if you leave. I want you to stay because you have chosen to live here for a while."

Mwende swallowed.

"You're very difficult, Mama."

The old woman laughed.

"You inherited that from your father."

At seven, Peter arrived anyway.

He found Mwende sitting at the dining table, staring at the flight details on her phone.

"You're still here."

"Yes."

He looked at the suitcase.

"What's the plan?"

She took a breath.

"I'm going to ask about changing the travel date. I want a few more weeks in Kenya before I make a longer-term work decision."

He sat down.

"Are you sure?"

"No."

Peter nodded.

"That's honest."

She smiled.

He looked at the house keys beside her tea.

"What about the house?"

"I want to spend some time there."

"Alone?"

"Sometimes."

"Won't you be bored?"

"Probably."

He laughed.

"What will you do?"

Mwende thought about her mother, about Wema and her book, about Faith's kitchen, about Ruth's receipts, about the little exercise book filled with skills she had almost forgotten she possessed.

"I'll find out," she said.

She contacted the airline and checked the options for changing her flight. There were costs, and she would need to settle her work arrangements properly. None of it was simple. She made the calls anyway.

By the time the original flight departed, Mwende was sitting in Faith's kitchen, cutting vegetables for a small lunch order.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Peter.

*The plane must be in the air now.*

She stared at it.

For one terrible second, panic rose through her chest. She imagined her seat occupied by a stranger, the meal trolley passing, Riyadh waiting beyond the clouds.

She had missed the familiar path.

What if she had made a mistake?

Faith noticed her face.

"What's wrong?"

"My flight has gone."

Faith put down the knife.

"Do you need to be on it?"

Mwende opened her mouth.

For seventeen years she had answered that question with money, obligation and fear.

Today she answered differently.

"Not today."

Faith nodded.

"Then cut the onions before they burn."

Mwende laughed, suddenly and helplessly, until tears ran down her face.

It was not a triumphant moment.

She had no new business.

No guarantee that she would earn enough in Kenya.

No promise that staying would become easy.

But she had made one decision that belonged to her.

And she had not needed somebody else's permission to make it.

## Epilogue: A Wednesday That Belonged to Her

Six weeks later, Mwende was still in Kenya.

The first two weeks had been difficult.

She woke too early. She checked her phone too often. She felt guilty when she sat without working. She worried about the salary she was not earning.

Some days she missed Riyadh sharply—the order of the household, the calls from Salma, the certainty of a task completed and a wage expected.

She did not stop missing it because she wanted to build a life in Kenya.

She did not have to hate one place in order to love another.

Her Machakos house now had a small table, a sofa bought second-hand and a set of curtains she had chosen herself, not for tenants or for photographs, but because she liked the pattern.

The kitchen contained her own cups.

There were groceries in the cupboard.

A half-finished novel lay on a chair.

Once, she went three days without cleaning every window.

The world did not end.

Faith was teaching her more about costing and keeping records. Mwende sometimes helped prepare orders and sometimes simply watched. They had discussed a small trial of paid meal preparation, but Mwende had refused to invest substantial savings before understanding the numbers and finding customers.

Peter had fixed the Syokimau fence after getting an agreed quotation. He sent photographs and receipts. Mwende thanked him without asking the same question three different ways.

Ruth came over on Sundays. Sometimes they worked on the accounts. Sometimes they drank tea and talked about things unrelated to money.

Wema now visited with a book tucked beneath her arm.

She no longer began by asking what Mwende had brought from Saudi Arabia.

One afternoon, she asked, "Auntie, what did you want to be when you were young?"

Mwende thought for a moment.

"A nurse."

"Why didn't you?"

"Life went another way."

Wema nodded seriously.

"Can it go a different way again?"

Mwende looked at her.

"Yes," she said slowly. "I think it can."

Her mother visited one Wednesday.

They sat on the veranda of the Machakos house, drinking tea while a breeze moved through the mango leaves.

Mama Kathini looked around.

"Your father would have liked this house."

Mwende smiled sadly.

"I know."

"He would have complained about the gate."

"He would."

"And he would have wanted a chair right here."

Mwende looked at the empty space beside them.

"Then we'll put one there."

Her mother reached for her hand.

For a long while they sat without talking.

Mwende no longer felt compelled to fill the silence with chores.

At four o'clock, her phone rang.

Madam Aisha.

Mwende answered.

They talked about the children, the house and her mother's health. They discussed the possibility of future work, but nothing was decided in that call.

Afterward, Mwende put the phone down.

Her mother watched her.

"Are you going back?"

"Maybe someday. If I need to, or if I choose to."

"And for now?"

Mwende looked out toward the gate.

Peter had promised to visit on Saturday. Wema wanted to finish their book. Faith had a catering order on Friday. She had a lesson on basic bookkeeping the following week.

There were still bills.

Still family disagreements.

Still mornings when she did not know what she was doing.

But the house no longer felt like a museum of sacrifices.

It was beginning to collect ordinary memories.

"For now," Mwende said, "I'm here."

Her mother nodded.

"Then make more tea."

Mwende rose, took two cups into the kitchen, and smiled at how familiar the request felt.

Only this time, she was not serving an employer.

She was making tea in her own home for a woman she loved.

Outside, an aeroplane crossed the afternoon sky.

Mwende heard it.

She did not look up.

**THE END**
