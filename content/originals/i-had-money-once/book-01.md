# I Had Money Once

*A fictionalised Kenyan city-life novella inspired by lived experiences. Characters and conversations are dramatised.*

VibeSchool Originals · Adult fiction · First edition · 2026

---

## Chapter 1: Friday, Midnight

By Friday midnight I was leaving Nairobi in a train so full of people and luggage that even the corridors looked occupied.

I had arrived on Monday at six in the morning.

Five days. That was enough time for me to come to the city, get a job, lose the job before starting it, and find myself asking my grandmother to send me fare home.

There is a photograph of me from those days, although I have never seen it. I carry it in my head. A young man in a navy-blue shirt, a green hood and safari boots. A backpack over one shoulder. His face still open to the world. His pockets not yet empty.

On the train that Friday, everybody appeared to have completed a successful mission. It was the holiday season and people were travelling towards western Kenya. They had sacks of maize, tins, sufurias, bags of omena, charcoal stoves and bundles tied with string. I saw a man trying to pass a stove over a woman's head without touching her hair. She complained before he even lifted it.

"Are you carrying a house or visiting your people?" she asked.

"These things are cheaper here," he said, as though that settled everything.

Someone laughed. A child began to cry. The smell of roasted maize travelled through the compartment. People called one another across the noise, negotiating space no one had.

They were carrying Nairobi home with them.

I had come to Nairobi carrying almost seven thousand shillings for my future. Now I was returning with a backpack, a full stomach from one mango, and a story that would sound like a lie.

I had passed the interview.

That was the part that disturbed me most.

A person can fail an interview and understand. He was not qualified. He answered badly. They chose somebody else. You return home and try again.

But I had passed. They told me so themselves. They had even taken money for my uniform.

I kept seeing the third-floor office, the chairs placed in a row, the faces of the people interviewing me. By Friday, the same room had become something else entirely.

A church had occupied it on Tuesday.

On Friday there was music and dancing.

I could not have invented a better way to be cheated.

The train jerked. People adjusted their bags. A woman beside me tied her leso more tightly around a sleeping child. Outside, the lights of Nairobi began moving past the windows.

There was nobody waiting for me at the other end with congratulations. I had not told the whole story at home. I could already imagine the questions.

How was the interview?

When are you reporting?

Where is the job?

I would have preferred someone to ask me about the weather.

The train gathered speed. The city moved away without saying goodbye.

I rested my head against the window and felt the cold from the glass.

When you are twenty-two, you think the worst thing that can happen is to fail in front of people. You have not yet learned the different kinds of failure a man can carry quietly.

I had no wife then. No child calling me Baba. No business worth a name. Not even a room in Nairobi where I could leave my bag.

I was just a boy from Kanyawa trying to become somebody.

To understand how I got onto that midnight train, you have to go back to the morning I came to the city.

And before that, to the silence at home.

## Chapter 2: The Last Young Man at Home

At home in Kanyawa, nobody held a meeting to tell me that I was wasting my life.

That would have been easier.

Instead, life continued around me. People woke early. Fires were lit. Water was fetched. Neighbours passed and asked the same ordinary questions. My age-mates were leaving one by one. A few had jobs. Some had gone to towns where there was work. Others had simply disappeared from the village and returned at Christmas wearing clothes that made them look as though the world had accepted them.

I remained.

It is possible to feel shame even among people who love you.

I would sit with a plate of food and think of what I should have brought into the house instead of eating what was there. When somebody asked what I planned to do, I had an answer. Several answers, actually. I always had plans.

That was one of my problems.

I could see a business where another person saw an empty shop. I could look at a crowd waiting for a service and imagine who might pay to make the waiting shorter. In my head, money had many roads. I had not yet learned how expensive it was to build even one.

School had left me with another wound. I had missed the mark I needed for the degree I wanted by one point. Computer science or motor vehicle engineering: I had imagined myself in the world of machines, solving things that ordinary people did not know how to solve.

One mark is a very small thing when written on paper.

It can be a very large thing when it stands between you and a life you have already imagined.

I had no godfather who could make a call and move the line. The result was the result.

People suggested other ways forward. I listened, but inside I kept returning to the same question. What could I do with the mind I had been given?

Then I saw the advertisement.

It was printed among the classifieds in a daily newspaper. Vacancies for turnboys and supermarket attendants. No big title, no promise of a corner office. Just work.

I read it several times.

A turnboy helped a driver load and unload goods. A supermarket attendant arranged shelves, served customers, did what needed doing. Neither job was my dream, but a first salary could become fare, rent and a beginning. A man could think about a bigger idea after buying his own supper.

I gathered the money I had.

No uncle in Nairobi said he would receive me. No cousin promised a bed. I did not know anybody who could show me around.

I decided to go anyway.

On the morning I left, my backpack seemed both too small and too heavy. It contained a few clothes, the things I thought a young man would need, and far more hope than could fit inside it.

I wore my navy-blue shirt, green hood and safari boots.

They were good boots for a man who believed he was going somewhere.

On the matatu, people spoke about fares, business, children, the price of things. I listened. Outside the window, familiar places gave way to places I knew only by name.

I told myself I would not return empty-handed.

That promise followed me all the way to Nairobi.

At six in the morning, we arrived in Nyamakima. The matatu doors opened and everybody stood at once. The people around me appeared to know the exact direction of their lives.

I let them go ahead.

Then I followed.

## Chapter 3: Monday, Six in the Morning

The people who got out of the matatu walked fast.

I walked fast too.

One man with a small suitcase turned right. I turned right. A woman crossed the road. The others scattered around her. I had no reason to believe any of them was going to the building near Afya Centre, but for a few minutes I preferred being wrong in company to being lost alone.

The crowd thinned without warning.

A man entered a shop. Two women climbed into another matatu. Someone ducked through a doorway. A boy carrying bread disappeared down a lane.

I stopped.

The people had known where they were going. I had only known where they were going up to that moment.

Nairobi kept moving. Buses groaned. Men shouted destinations. Someone pushed a trolley between pedestrians who did not seem interested in giving way. People passed my navy-blue shirt and backpack without a second look.

The city did not ask where I had come from.

That was the first thing I noticed about Nairobi. In Kanyawa, people knew when you arrived, when you left and sometimes where you ought to have been. Here, you could stand among a thousand people and belong to none of them.

I found my way by asking, checking, walking and asking again.

Near Afya Centre I located the building. The office was on the third floor. Three floors were not much when a job was waiting at the top.

Inside were chairs and people who looked busy. There were papers, questions, the ordinary language of employment. Nothing that warned me to run.

I had been afraid that the interview might expose how little I knew. Instead I answered clearly. I could work hard, I said. I could learn. I could report when required. I did not come to Nairobi to refuse a job because it was beneath me.

The person interviewing me made marks on a paper.

After some waiting, I was told I had passed.

I remember the relief more than the exact words.

There is a moment when good news reaches your ears before your mind believes it. For a few seconds I felt tall. The journey from Kanyawa had been worth it. My age-mates could have their own stories. I now had mine.

Then came the arrangements.

The uniform had to be paid for. Accommodation would be organised. There were other errands the agency had to handle before I could begin work. Each thing sounded reasonable when explained by somebody sitting behind an office desk.

I paid for one thing and then another.

By the time I left, almost seven thousand shillings had passed out of my hands.

I watched it go with the cheerful pain of a man spending on his future. I was not buying a shirt from a hawker. I was preparing to earn a salary. In my mind, the money was already returning in the first month.

They said I should come back the next day for the appointment.

I stepped into the street and smiled without meaning to.

I looked at the buildings differently. Yesterday Nairobi had been somewhere other people went. Now it was where I was about to work.

I thought of home. I considered how much of my first salary I might send there. Not a large amount, but enough to show that leaving had been a sensible decision.

Nobody had told me where to sleep that night.

That detail had seemed unimportant while I was sitting in the office.

Outside, surrounded by people rushing to places of their own, it began to grow.

## Chapter 4: The Office That Became a Church

On Tuesday I climbed back to the third floor.

I was early enough to feel responsible and late enough to find people already inside.

Only they were not the people I had met on Monday.

The room was being used as a church.

I stood at the entrance, looking past the doorway.

I knew the room. I knew the floor. I knew the building. Yesterday I had sat there and answered questions about a job. Now the place had a different purpose.

For a moment I thought the employment people had moved the office.

"Excuse me," I said. "The agency that was here yesterday?"

A person looked at me with the mild surprise you give a stranger who has asked for the wrong house.

"Which agency?"

I described it. Interviews. Turnboy positions. Supermarket jobs. The money for uniforms and accommodation.

Nobody knew what I was talking about.

I went outside and looked at the building again. I asked more questions. I went back upstairs. I tried to remember the faces from Monday, the arrangement of chairs, the place where I had handed over my money.

It was all real.

Yet in front of me there was no office.

People tell you to trust your eyes. That day I discovered that eyes can give evidence which the following day refuses to support.

I waited for someone to arrive and explain.

Nobody came.

By afternoon my confidence had become a collection of small excuses. Perhaps I had misunderstood the date. Perhaps the agency shared the room with the church. Perhaps business people used different offices on different days. I was young enough to think every problem had an explanation if I kept looking.

I returned to the building as the week went on.

On Friday, four days after the interview, music was coming from the same room. It was a dance hall now, a jam session. People moved to the rhythm, laughing and calling across the noise.

A whole office had turned into a church and then a place for dancing.

I had been employed by a room.

And the room had moved on without me.

I stood there long enough for the truth to settle.

There would be no appointment letter. No uniform. No accommodation. No first salary.

I had been cheated.

I did not know the names behind the business, whether they had rented the space for a few hours or how many other young people had paid them. I only knew what had happened to my money.

It had disappeared as quickly as the chairs.

The rest of my stay in Nairobi was not one long disaster. It was a series of ordinary hours made difficult by having no home there.

I walked. I asked about work. I checked the little money left. I thought about the things I could do with a computer if somebody would let me. I watched people buy food without first calculating the next meal.

At Nairobi Railway Station, I found a train compartment where I could rest.

The mosquitoes were merciless.

They reached the parts of me the green hood could not cover. I pulled at my clothes, shifted from one position to another and slapped the darkness when I heard them near my ears.

I had travelled to Nairobi to become a salaried man. Now I was bargaining with mosquitoes for a few hours of sleep.

I remember the hard place where I lay. I remember my backpack close to me. I remember waking suddenly whenever I heard a sound.

The thing about having nowhere to sleep is that the night becomes very long. In the daytime the city offers movement. You can walk as though you have somewhere to be. At night it begins asking questions.

Whose door will open for you?

Who knows you are here?

If something happens, who will notice?

I had no answer.

I saw people who had made the streets their living place. I do not know their stories. Perhaps some had come to Nairobi with better plans than mine. I felt how thin the difference could be between us.

But something in me resisted staying there.

This was not courage in the way people use that word in speeches. I was frightened. I was embarrassed. I simply could not accept that five days should decide the rest of my life.

On Friday I made my choice.

I would go home.

Then I remembered the small problem I had been trying not to think about.

I could not afford the journey.

## Chapter 5: The Mango and the Midnight Train

The person who helped me first was not a businessman, a government officer or one of the people who had promised me work.

It was a girl I had known in high school.

I met her around the matatus. She knew me before Nairobi had turned my face into that of a man looking for something he had lost.

We talked. I do not remember every word. Some conversations stay with you as a feeling rather than a transcript.

I remember that she gave me two hundred shillings.

Two hundred was not enough to repair what had happened. It was enough to remind me that I had not disappeared completely from the world.

I bought an embe dodo, a mango.

People who have never had to count the price of a meal may not understand why I remember the mango. A hundred larger things have happened to me since then. I have handled more money than I carried to Nairobi that Monday. I have sold goods, organised services and planned businesses that could have employed many people.

Yet the mango has remained.

There are meals you forget while still eating them. There are others you remember for the rest of your life.

I then went to a simu ya jamii, one of those public telephone businesses where a person kept a phone and charged you to call.

I called my grandmother.

A grandmother can hear what you are hiding even when the line is poor.

I explained enough for her to understand that I needed fare home.

She sent it.

That was how my return journey became possible.

By the time I reached the station on Friday night, I was exhausted in a way sleep could not repair. The train was already filling with people travelling west for the holidays.

A man carried a sack of maize as if it were a sick relative. Another watched over a cooking stove. Women guarded bags of food. Children slept and woke and slept again. Omena added its own voice to the smells inside the carriage.

For most of the passengers, home was a place they were returning to with presents and goods from Nairobi.

For me, home was a place where I would have to answer questions.

I had a place on that train because my grandmother had sent money. I had eaten because a former girlfriend had given me two hundred shillings.

The people I had paid almost seven thousand shillings for employment were nowhere to be found.

I thought about it for a long time.

At one stop, a passenger climbed down and bought tea through a window. Someone argued about a seat. The train blew its horn and moved on.

I stared outside.

I had promised myself I would not go back empty-handed. Now I had no choice. I could not eat a promise and I could not sleep inside it.

I was returning to Kanyawa.

I imagined walking into the compound with the same backpack. It would be lighter in one way and heavier in another.

I decided I would tell the truth. Perhaps not all at once, and perhaps not to everybody, but I would not invent a salary or a job that did not exist.

The train carried me through the night.

I did not know that in the years ahead I would work around funerals, discover business in places other people avoided, make and lose money, become a husband and a father, and have children who could make me return home merely by calling my name.

Back then, there was only one thing I was sure of.

I would return to Nairobi.

Not for that office.

Not for that uniform.

I would return for the life I had gone there to find.

## Chapter 6: The Business of Other People's Goodbyes

Back home, the hardest thing was not explaining how I had lost the money.

It was waking up the following morning and still being the same young man.

The compound was familiar. The voices were familiar. The work that needed doing was familiar. Nairobi had changed something inside me, but the world around me had not made any special arrangement for that change.

I began looking for what I could do.

At one point I tried my luck with recruitment into the forces. Other young men talked about connections, people who could speak for them. I did not have anyone like that.

I failed to get into the army.

The following day there was police recruitment. I went there too.

When I was asked what I wanted, I said I wanted to join Kenya Traffic, not the police.

I did not know the difference.

Years later, I could tell that story and laugh until my sides hurt. At the time, it was another door closing while I still stood in front of it.

But the mind that had carried me to Nairobi had not stopped searching.

I noticed businesses other people passed without looking twice.

Death, for example.

Death was not a business people liked discussing in cheerful company. But when a person died, a family had many urgent needs. A coffin was needed. Transport was needed. Relatives had to be informed. There were arrangements that could not wait until the family finished grieving.

I began selling coffins and arranging hearse services.

Some customers spoke quietly as though any conversation about prices would offend the person who had died. Others negotiated fiercely. Grief did not make everybody the same.

I learned to listen.

At one funeral a man could spend time arguing over a small difference in cost. At another, a wealthy family might ask for something to be ready that afternoon and pay because they wanted the problem removed from their hands.

I started seeing where other services fitted.

Eulogies.

People needed programmes with a life story, photographs, names, dates, tributes and an order of service. At wealthy funerals the printing mattered. A poor design could embarrass a family at the very moment when everybody was looking.

I learned how to prepare and sell them.

Sometimes I stood among people who had arrived in polished cars, listening to speeches about a man whose life had been full of achievements. Then I would return to my own circumstances, counting the money I had earned by helping them say goodbye.

It was a strange classroom.

The dead had completed their lives. The living were still bargaining over the cost of telling the story.

I did not make a fortune each time. Some jobs paid well. Some consumed too much time. Some people changed their minds after work had started.

Still, there was money to be made.

The cybercafés opened another road.

I understood computers when many people around me were still afraid of pressing the wrong key. I could prepare documents, help with online services, solve a problem at a machine and receive a commission.

I did not always need to own the computer to earn from knowing how to use it.

That felt important.

At school I had missed a mark. Out here I could do work that the mark had supposedly kept me away from.

But there was one weakness in the arrangement.

A cybercafé belonged to somebody else. My commission depended on the next customer, the next favour and the patience of the person who owned the place.

And there were periods when I still did not have a reliable place to sleep.

I could earn in the morning and spend the evening wondering where I would put my head.

It made me impatient.

I wanted something of my own. Not simply a job, but a system. A business that worked even when I was not standing beside it. Something that could begin small, spread to another town, then another, until the problem of tomorrow's meal was no longer the only problem I had room to consider.

There were moments when I almost reached it.

Then money would be needed for equipment, premises, people, transport, stock. The idea would work in one corner but fail when it tried to become larger.

I learned to sell a service.

I had not yet learned how to build a business that could outlive my own effort.

And I kept making the mistake of thinking the next good idea would solve that problem.

## Chapter 7: The Woman I Married

I did not marry because I had reached the place in life where a man calmly decides that he is ready.

I married because a woman became pregnant and I believed I had to take responsibility.

There are men who run away from that moment. I did not want to be one of them.

The trouble was that I confused doing the responsible thing with knowing how to build a marriage.

Those are not the same skill.

We had not begun with the kind of love I later understood a home needed. I told myself that affection could grow and that duty would carry whatever feeling could not. A child was coming. There was rent to think about, food to buy, visits, medicine, all the small expenses that become a mountain when they arrive together.

I was still the man who could see ten businesses in an empty room.

She needed a man who could tell her what money would be available on Friday.

I could describe a future that would make us comfortable. She was asking about the present, where the landlord lived and the shopkeeper wanted payment.

Neither question was foolish.

But we answered each other badly.

When I talked about a business opportunity, she sometimes heard another risk. When she asked about money, I sometimes heard that she did not believe in me.

A small conversation could become a quarrel before either of us reached the thing we wanted to say.

"How much will this bring?" she would ask.

"It can become big," I would answer.

"I am asking about this month."

"I am telling you what it can become."

Perhaps that was the sentence that kept us apart: I spoke in what could become; she spoke in what was already due.

I was not always fair to her.

It is easy for a man with big plans to treat caution as an insult. I sometimes made a promise because I could imagine the money coming, not because the money was in my hand. When it did not arrive, the disappointment belonged to both of us.

And she was not always fair to me.

There were words and decisions that wounded me. We hurt one another in ways that did not look serious to people outside the house. A marriage does not have to make a noise for the people inside it to feel trapped.

I discovered that being responsible for a family could make me afraid of the very risk I had once welcomed.

Before marriage, I could fail and sleep hungry. I had done it before.

Now a failed business could mean a child without something necessary, a tense evening at home, a bill I could not meet.

I began protecting the household by delaying the things I believed might build a better household.

Then I resented the delay.

I am not proud of every choice I made during those years.

I had gone into the marriage hoping responsibility would produce love. Instead, pressure revealed how little we knew about each other.

Eventually, we separated.

I will not pretend I felt only relief. A marriage can fail and still leave behind good memories, mistakes you regret, and people whose lives remain tied to yours.

There were children. There was history. There were things that could not be packed into a bag and carried away.

But something changed after she left.

The fear that had been sitting on my shoulders grew lighter.

I could take risks again without hearing the sound of a whole household falling if I made the wrong move. I began earning better. I worked with a freedom I had almost forgotten.

It would be simple to say the woman had been the reason I was not succeeding.

It would also be false.

Our marriage had become a place where my weaknesses and hers met every day. When it ended, I was forced to see my own share of what had gone wrong.

I had been brave enough to take responsibility for a pregnancy.

I had not been wise enough to understand the difference between a promise and a plan.

The children taught me that difference later.

## Chapter 8: I Had Money Once

There were years when I had money.

Not the kind you see in photographs of people standing beside aircraft. Real money. Money I had earned by noticing an opportunity and putting people, tools and services together. Enough to stop thinking about every fare before boarding a vehicle. Enough to start believing I had finally crossed into a different part of life.

The strange thing about making money after a period of hardship is that you do not immediately become comfortable.

You continue thinking like a man with none.

You inspect opportunities more quickly. You count what others waste. Sometimes you spend too freely just to prove to yourself that the old days are over.

I found that making the first money was not always my difficulty.

Keeping a business alive after its first success was.

I could see how a service might work in one town and then in many. I could explain the model to people until their eyes changed and they began to see what I saw.

"This can work," they would say.

"I know," I would answer.

Then we would reach the part where something had to be paid for before the next customer arrived.

That was when the room grew quiet.

A larger office. Equipment. More hands. Software. Transport. A proper place from which the work could be done.

A small business can survive on the owner's strength. A growing one needs more than strength.

Some people liked my ideas until the ideas needed their money. Others joined with excitement and left when the work became ordinary. A few stayed long enough to teach me what trust actually cost.

I began to understand that belief and commitment were two separate things.

It is easy to believe in a bridge while standing on the bank. It is another thing to carry stones into the river.

There was another problem.

Sometimes I loved the beginning too much.

The first customer. The first proof that something could be done. The first time a person said, "You were right."

Those moments made me restless for a bigger version.

I would begin planning the second town before the first one had paid all its bills.

An idea does not fail only because it is bad. It can fail because it grows faster than the money holding it up.

I had watched this happen more than once.

I knew it.

Then I would see another possibility and feel the old excitement returning.

People who cared about me sometimes thought I was chasing dreams to avoid hard work.

They were wrong about the work. I could work.

But they were not completely wrong about the dreams.

I had to learn how to stay with one thing after the clever part was finished and the dull part had begun.

Accounts. Collections. Following up. Paying small bills before they became large ones. Making promises only after counting what was in the bank. Building a business that did not need my mouth to explain it to every new person.

Nobody claps when you reconcile accounts. Nobody calls you a visionary for keeping a receipt. Yet those things can save what a brilliant idea starts.

At times I found myself around Utawala, near the GSU area, and the memory of Nairobi's streets returned.

I remembered the nights without a proper bed.

I remembered the railway compartment and the mosquitoes.

I remembered the third-floor office and the way I had handed over nearly seven thousand shillings because a stranger said a uniform was waiting for me.

Even when money came, those memories could put cold in my stomach.

I was afraid of falling all the way back.

The fear sometimes made me careful.

Other times it made me rush, because I wanted so badly to put enough distance between myself and that young man with the backpack.

I had money once.

I had lost some of it.

I had made some again.

The lesson was not that money did not matter. I knew exactly how much it mattered when there was no fare and no place to sleep.

The lesson was that money could leave a man whose mind was still full of plans.

So I stopped asking only how to earn it.

I began asking how to keep the things I built from falling apart when the next hard season arrived.

## Chapter 9: Baba

These days, I can be working on an idea and hear my name from the other room.

"Baba!"

I usually know which child it is before I see the face.

Sometimes the voice is demanding. Sometimes offended. Sometimes delighted by something I will have to examine seriously, even if it is only a drawing or a broken toy.

There are five children in my life. Some are mine by blood. Some came into the family through another marriage. Children do not study our arrangements the way adults do. They study whether you show up.

When I leave, there is often a small protest.

"Baba, where are you going?"

One tries to hold my hand. Another comes running because the first has raised the alarm. Soon my name is moving around the house as if I am a very important visitor who must not be allowed to escape.

I used to think being important meant somebody in an office knew my name.

Now I know a smaller audience can make greater demands.

I became a father before I was ready. If you had asked me to show you a plan for raising children, I would have given you one of my good speeches about the future.

Real children did not wait for the speech to end.

They needed meals, shoes, attention, medicine, stories and the simple assurance that I would return after going out.

I have given up things I once wanted so that they can have a better chance than I had. Not everything. The old dream remains. But it has changed shape.

When I was twenty-two, I wanted to make people at home proud that I had made it in Nairobi.

Now I want the children to have choices when I am gone.

I do not expect every man to believe that having children young is a good thing. It can be hard. I know the cost. I have paid some of it with opportunities I could not pursue, and with nights spent calculating money instead of sleeping.

For me, though, the children made the future a person instead of an idea.

The youngest does not care about the size of my business. The child knows whether I was there yesterday.

There are evenings when I sit with a notebook or a screen and start drawing the system I still want to build. I think about people who are denied opportunities because of where they were born, because there was no person to make a call for them, because one mark was missing, because a family could not pay.

I think about how information, a service or a lesson might reach them without waiting for somebody powerful to open a door.

I can see it clearly.

That is the blessing and the danger of my mind.

I see the whole thing before I have built the first reliable part.

I have begun trying to do something different. One page. One customer. One completed task. One bill paid. Then the next.

It sounds small for a man who has always spoken about scale.

But I have lived long enough to know that a small thing which survives can become bigger than a grand thing which collapses.

One evening I opened an old bag and found a paper folded among things I had kept for years.

It was not the original newspaper advertisement. I had lost that long ago. It was a scrap on which I had once written an address in Nairobi.

I looked at it for a long time.

Near Afya Centre. Third floor.

I laughed.

If those people could see me now, they might not recognise me. The young man who paid them for a uniform had grown older. He had lived through a marriage, a separation, businesses that worked and businesses that failed. He had learned things about money that they could not have sold him.

I wondered whether I would still follow the crowd from Nyamakima if I arrived again at six in the morning.

Probably not.

Then I thought of the young man himself.

He had no idea what was coming. No idea about the railway station, the recruiting lines, the coffins, the cybercafés, the eulogies, the children, the hours of work with little to show for them. No idea how many times he would begin again.

And yet he had boarded that matatu.

I cannot laugh at him too much.

He brought me here.

From the other room came the familiar cry.

"Baba! Baba! Baba!"

I closed the notebook.

At the doorway, the children were waiting, each with a different reason for wanting me.

I stood for a moment, looking at them.

In the morning I would return to my work. There were calls to make, a plan to finish, people who might say yes and others who would say no. I still wanted to build something large enough to last beyond me.

I was not finished.

But that evening, I did not make them wait.

I went in.

Behind me, on the table, the notebook lay open to a page with three words written at the top.

START WITH ONE.

And for the first time in a long while, the plan beneath those words was small enough to begin tomorrow.
