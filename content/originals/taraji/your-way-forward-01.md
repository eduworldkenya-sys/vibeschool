# Your Way Forward — Taraji's Story

VibeSchool Originals · Book One · English · Text-only edition

## Chapter 1: Seventeen Minutes Late

Taraji was late for class.

This would have been less embarrassing if anybody besides Taraji had been expecting her.

At 8:17 on Monday morning, she sat on a plastic chair in her mother's kitchen, eating cold chapati and telling her best friend that yes, she had considered the consequences of not joining college.

"All of them?" Asha asked.

"The important ones."

"Name three."

Taraji swallowed. "You sound like a lecturer."

"Name one."

"Transport. I save transport."

"Good. Next."

"Fees."

"Obviously."

"And I get to eat this very excellent chapati without paying canteen prices."

Asha laughed so loudly that Taraji held the phone away from her ear.

Across the room, the accounting book lay open at page thirty-one. Taraji had read page thirty-one twice the previous night. Both readings had given her the impression that the author was angry with her personally.

"Don't you have a lecture?" Taraji asked.

"I'm outside the building. We have ten minutes."

"Then go and make friends."

"I have friends. One of them has declared herself a college."

"A small institution with ambitious plans."

There was a pause.

"Taraji," Asha said, lowering her voice, "you know I'm not saying you can't do it."

"That's exactly what you're saying, just wearing a nice blouse."

"I'm saying you've made a hard thing harder."

Taraji looked at the timetable taped above the sink. It was written in three colours, and it had already been amended twice. At eight she was supposed to be studying. At nine she was supposed to be testing herself. At ten she was due at Kavu Print, where she worked part-time and where Mr Otieno had developed the ability to identify lateness by the sound of a person's footsteps.

"Maybe," she said.

That was more agreement than Asha had expected.

Taraji's phone buzzed. Her mother had sent a message from the tailoring stall.

We talk tonight. Properly. No jokes.

Underneath it was another.

And buy tomatoes.

Taraji sent a thumbs-up. She knew which of the two instructions was more urgent.

The morning did not improve. At eight-thirty she opened the book. At eight-forty she watched a video explaining a topic she had not yet reached. At nine she reorganised her notes into attractive headings. At nine-fifteen she noticed that all she had accomplished was a cleaner notebook.

She wrote on a scrap of paper:

What can I do now that I couldn't do yesterday?

Then she stared at the question.

At Kavu Print, Mr Otieno gave her a job collating forty-seven sets of conference handouts. She worked quickly, talking to a customer about his new business while keeping count with one hand. By noon she had finished thirty-six sets and made one man laugh so hard that he forgot what he had come to photocopy.

"You're in a good mood," Mr Otieno observed.

"I am an educated woman."

"You have been educated since yesterday?"

"I've started CPA."

He examined the paper sets. "Where?"

"Independently."

"Ah." He said it the way people said ah when a mechanic told them their engine needed replacing.

Taraji waited for the rest.

"Well," he said, "whatever school you're attending, tell them to teach you to count. Set twelve has two copies of page seven."

She checked. He was right.

On the way home she bought tomatoes, two onions and a packet of salt she hadn't been asked for. She took the long road past the college where Asha studied. Students were spilling through the gate, laughing in clusters and arguing about an assignment. One boy balanced a ruler behind his ear. Another complained that the lecturer had rushed through everything.

Taraji watched them for a moment.

She wanted the freedom to be her own kind of student. She had not expected to miss the noise.

At home, her mother was waiting with a small exercise book.

"Sit," she said.

Taraji sat.

Her mother opened the book to a page headed HOUSEHOLD, in capitals, and slid it across the table.

"We are not discussing whether you are clever," she said. "I know you are. We are discussing how this decision is going to work."

Taraji had prepared three speeches. None began with numbers.

For the first time all day, she closed her mouth and listened.

## Chapter 2: The Price of a Desk

Grace, Taraji's mother, kept accounts in a blue exercise book that had lost its cover years ago. Every school expense, every material she bought for her sewing business, every contribution her relatives expected appeared there in unusually tidy handwriting.

Taraji had inherited the confidence. The tidiness had gone to her younger brother, Lenny.

"College fees," Grace said, tapping one line. "Transport. Lunch. Books. Now show me what you plan to spend instead."

"I won't need transport every day."

"Write zero, then. We can argue later."

Taraji wrote it down.

"Internet?"

"Yes."

"Examination fees?"

"Yes."

"Books?"

"Some."

"Electricity?"

"Mum, we already have electricity."

"Does it work for free when you use it?"

Lenny, who was ostensibly doing homework at the opposite end of the table, snorted.

Taraji drew a line underneath the figures.

The savings were real. So were the new expenses. Her phone screen had a crack shaped like the letter Y, and it froze whenever she opened a document longer than six pages. Her work at Kavu Print was irregular. Some weeks Mr Otieno needed her every afternoon. Other weeks, not at all.

Grace waited until Taraji stopped calculating.

"I don't mind you choosing a different road," she said. "I mind you pretending roads don't have potholes."

"Some have very beautiful potholes."

"Please don't."

Taraji put the pencil down.

"I can save something from my wages every week. I can use the public library on days when the house is noisy. I'll register for the papers I'm ready for, not the ones that make me look impressive."

Grace nodded once.

"And when you don't understand?"

"I'll look for help."

"Where?"

That question was less comfortable.

Taraji had spent the past month explaining why she did not need a lecturer. She had not spent much time finding anyone who might answer a question at seven in the evening.

"Still working on that."

"Good. Put it on your list."

They agreed on a review after twelve weeks: money spent, work completed, subjects understood and what Taraji would change. Not a vote on whether she deserved freedom. A chance to examine whether the way she had chosen was working.

Later, Taraji lay on her bed listening to Lenny play a football game through the thin wall. Asha sent a photograph from campus: five students crowded around a plate of chips, each pointing accusingly at a different person.

You'd like these idiots, Asha wrote.

Taraji typed, I have my own idiots.

She deleted it.

She did not, yet.

The following day, she went to the library. The librarian, Mrs Wairimu, had been there for as long as Taraji could remember. She could find a mis-shelved book without looking up from her desk, a talent that Taraji considered slightly supernatural.

"Accounting?" Mrs Wairimu said, taking in the calculator, notebook and determined expression.

"How did you know?"

"Because nobody brings a calculator here to read a romance."

Taraji found a seat near the window. Beside her was a man marking pages in a thick folder. Across the table a young woman in a salon apron reviewed handwritten notes while eating a banana.

Nobody spoke. Nobody knew she was beginning something difficult. Nobody had assigned her that chair.

She found she liked that.

Forty minutes later she was stuck on an exercise. She tried three approaches and arrived at three answers, all wrong in different directions.

The woman opposite looked up. "You're pressing that calculator as though it insulted your mother."

"It might have."

"Let me see."

Taraji hesitated. She was so accustomed to announcing her independence that accepting help felt, for one foolish second, like a betrayal.

The woman pulled the notebook towards her.

"I'm Nuru," she said. "I work at the salon opposite the stage. I understand this one because I got it wrong so many times."

She explained the mistake in two minutes.

Taraji looked from her page to Nuru. "Are you doing CPA?"

"Trying. If I don't fall asleep in the middle of every chapter."

Taraji laughed.

When she returned home, she added a line beneath her budget.

People I can ask.

For the first time, the list wasn't entirely empty.

## Chapter 3: The Art of Being Busy

Taraji had a talent for making a day look successful.

She could fill six pages with notes, answer seventeen messages, organise a shelf, visit the library, make tea for the entire household and still have no idea how to solve the question she had started with.

On Wednesday she developed a new system involving coloured sticky notes.

By Thursday the sticky notes had their own system.

On Friday, Asha arrived without warning.

"Why are there arrows on your wardrobe?"

"Revision map."

"And why does one point to your shoes?"

"That's the cash flow section."

Asha stood very still. "I'm not even going to ask."

They took tea on the small balcony. The neighbour below was arguing with somebody about a missing cooking pot. A radio played loudly behind them. A motorbike passed, revving as though preparing for flight.

Asha had an assignment to finish. Taraji offered to help, mostly because she wanted to prove she could keep up with students who had lecturers.

They opened the same topic, each using her own material.

Asha wrote an answer almost immediately.

Taraji stared at it. "Why are you treating that as an expense?"

"Because it is."

"It's paid in advance."

Asha frowned and checked the question.

For a delicious thirty seconds, Taraji was right.

Then Asha pointed to the second sentence, which Taraji had missed. They checked the workings together and discovered that both had made different errors.

"Very encouraging," Asha said.

"Two future accountants, one wrong answer."

"Perhaps we could open a business selling confidence."

"That, we have in stock."

They began again, slowly. They wrote down why each entry belonged where it did. When they finished, they closed their notes and attempted a similar question from memory.

Taraji got further than she had all week.

"That's annoying," she said.

"What?"

"Actual work works."

Asha looked around the balcony, taking in the beautifully colour-coded wall through the open door.

"Try to remain calm."

But by Sunday Taraji's attention had wandered again. An invitation to a birthday lunch became a whole afternoon. Her cousin needed help designing a poster. A video she had opened to explain one formula led to another about small businesses, then another about a woman who bought a house before thirty.

At nine in the evening, Taraji found herself calculating mortgage payments for a house she did not own, with money she had not earned, instead of studying for an exam she intended to sit.

She put her phone face down.

What can I do now that I couldn't do yesterday?

The scrap of paper was still under her notebook.

Not much, she admitted.

For once she did not write a fresh timetable. She took the question she had been avoiding all week, worked through the first step and marked where she became confused.

Then she sent Nuru a photograph.

Does this first part make sense?

Nuru replied with a voice note.

The first half was clear. The second half contained the unmistakable sound of a hairdryer and someone shouting that the customer had been waiting since four.

"Your first step is fine," Nuru said. "But look at what happens after the adjustment. Also, don't send me a full textbook at night. I have a life."

Taraji smiled.

She wrote beneath the question:

First step understood. Next step tomorrow.

It was a smaller victory than a wardrobe covered in plans.

It was also the first one she could prove.

## Chapter 4: A Seat at Somebody Else's Table

Asha's college had an open study afternoon on Saturdays. She invited Taraji.

"Is this a trap?" Taraji asked.

"Yes. We lure independent students in and make them fill forms."

"Terrible institution."

"Come anyway."

Taraji wore a yellow shirt, carried a notebook and arrived twenty minutes early, which she considered a personal achievement worth recording.

The campus was louder than she expected. People knew where to go. They waved across corridors and interrupted conversations without apology. A student called out to Asha from a balcony. Another tried to borrow a pen she did not have.

Taraji liked the disorder immediately.

At a long table outside the library sat four students with papers spread between juice bottles. Asha introduced her.

"This is Taraji. She's doing CPA on her own."

One student, a man in a checked shirt, looked up. "So who's teaching you?"

Taraji leaned on the chair. "Depends on the topic. Sometimes a book, sometimes a video, sometimes someone who explains it better than the book."

He shrugged. "Wouldn't work for me."

"Then don't do it."

The exchange might have ended there, but a woman at the end of the table began laughing.

"Finally," she said. "Someone who doesn't need to recruit the whole country into her choices."

Her name was Biko. She attended evening lectures after working mornings at a pharmacy, and she had a way of speaking softly that made other people stop talking to hear her.

The student in the checked shirt, Kelvin, pushed an exercise towards Taraji. "Fine. Solve that one."

It was less an invitation than a challenge. Taraji felt the familiar temptation to perform. She wanted to take the paper, solve the problem in a flourish and make everybody admit that she was doing brilliantly without college.

She read the first line.

She could not solve it.

Not quickly, anyway.

"I'd need to work through that," she said.

Kelvin smirked. "Exactly."

Asha's face hardened, but Taraji put down her bag and pulled out a chair.

"Do you know it?"

"Of course."

"Then let's see how you got there."

Kelvin started talking. Halfway through he noticed a number he had copied incorrectly. Biko pointed to another error. Asha checked a definition against the material. Ten minutes later they had a sensible method and a result everybody could explain.

Kelvin looked mildly disappointed that nobody had won the argument.

Taraji found the whole thing unexpectedly pleasant.

In the corridor afterwards, Asha said, "You handled him well."

"I wanted to throw the calculator at his forehead."

"Also reasonable."

"No. Expensive calculator."

They stopped at the noticeboard. There were posters for sports, a public-speaking event, a professional talk and something called STUDENT WELLNESS WEEK that featured three smiling people holding oranges.

Taraji read them with more interest than she cared to admit.

Asha watched her.

"You miss this part," she said.

"Which part?"

"The people."

Taraji didn't argue.

"I don't want your timetable," she said. "I don't want to be here every day. But I like knowing there are people I can sit with and learn from."

Asha nodded. "Then come when you want. You're allowed."

That evening Taraji messaged Nuru.

I met some other CPA students. Thinking we could study together sometimes. Different places, different schedules. No compulsory speeches.

Nuru replied:

As long as nobody appoints you principal.

Taraji looked at the message for a long time.

Then she typed:

Too late. I've already chosen the school motto.

Nuru sent back a single word.

Don't.
