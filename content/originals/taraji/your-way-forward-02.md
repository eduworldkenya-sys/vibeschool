## Chapter 5: The Tuesday Table

The first meeting of Taraji's study group lasted nine minutes.

The second lasted two hours.

This was because the first had been scheduled for six in the evening, when Nuru was still removing braids from a customer who kept changing her mind about what she wanted, Biko was held at the pharmacy and Asha was stuck in traffic.

Taraji sat alone at a table outside a tea shop, guarding four plastic chairs with the expression of somebody who had been stood up by an entire wedding party.

At 6:09 she ordered tea.

At 6:11 the waiter took away two chairs.

At 6:13 she decided she was finished building community.

Nuru arrived at 6:17 carrying a handbag, a calculator and a banana.

"Sorry," she said. "The hair had opinions."

Taraji stood. "You're late."

"So are you. To whatever exam you're worrying about."

Nuru sat down, took a bite of the banana and examined the notebook Taraji had laid out.

"Are we forming a group or applying for a loan?"

The page contained a timetable, a proposed attendance register, topics for discussion and a box labelled OBJECTIVES.

Taraji tore it out.

"What are you doing?" Nuru asked.

"Improving the constitution."

Biko arrived with his pharmacy name tag still clipped to his shirt. Asha came ten minutes later, apologising to everybody in advance. Kelvin appeared without being invited, carrying the exercise he had challenged Taraji with on Saturday.

"Your friend told me there'd be questions," he said.

"There will," Taraji said. "Some of them about why you're here."

He pulled up a chair.

For the first ten minutes they tried to agree on a schedule. Biko could do Tuesdays only if his shift ended early. Nuru had Sundays free but helped her aunt that day. Asha had lectures and assignments. Kelvin wanted everyone to meet at six in the morning, which was rejected with a level of unity the group would never again achieve.

"Why are we planning for five people to become the same person?" Nuru asked.

They went quiet.

She opened her notebook.

"I have a question I can't solve. You have one, she has one, he has twelve opinions. Why not start with that?"

So they did.

They argued through a problem, discovered that Asha's lecturer had explained one part particularly well, and found that Biko had a method for checking mistakes that none of them had tried. Kelvin, when he wasn't competing with imaginary opponents, could explain difficult material with surprising patience.

Taraji was laughing when she realised the tea shop had begun stacking chairs around them.

On her way home she counted the things that had gone wrong. The meeting had started late. Nobody had followed the timetable. They had covered only one topic.

It was the best study session she'd had in weeks.

She named the WhatsApp group Tuesday Table. Nuru changed the group picture to a photograph of a cracked plastic table. Biko pinned a message asking people not to forward unrelated videos. Kelvin objected that the picture made them look poor.

"We're students," Asha replied. "What did you expect?"

Taraji began typing a welcome paragraph. It was four hundred words long.

She read it back, deleted it and replaced it with:

Bring a question. Leave with something you understand.

Nuru reacted with a laughing sticker.

By midnight someone had already sent a question.

And for the first time since Taraji had decided not to join college, there was a room—small, noisy, difficult to organise—in which she was expected.

## Chapter 6: Thirty-Eight

Taraji got thirty-eight percent in her first timed practice test.

She looked at the result, closed the document and opened it again, as if the numbers might have rearranged themselves while she wasn't watching.

They hadn't.

Thirty-eight.

She had answered some questions confidently. Those worried her most now. She had also spent eleven minutes on one calculation, partly because she couldn't remember the method and partly because it seemed rude to abandon something she'd already spent eleven minutes on.

She sent the score to no one.

At Kavu Print the next morning she made three mistakes in a single order. Mr Otieno looked at the pages in silence.

"Are you ill?"

"No."

"Then what's going on?"

"Nothing."

He placed the pages beside her calculator. "You do understand that I pay you for the correct ones?"

Taraji started again, angry with him and then angrier with herself.

That evening the group was discussing their practice results. Asha had done well but was upset about a topic she kept forgetting. Biko had completed half the paper after a double shift. Nuru had written, I did fifteen minutes and then slept face-down on my notes. Even Kelvin confessed he'd left two questions blank.

Taraji contributed a joke and disappeared from the chat.

Asha called.

"What did you get?"

"Let's discuss something else."

"Taraji."

"Thirty-eight."

There was a pause, then the ordinary sound of Asha putting down a cup.

"Okay," she said.

"That's all? Okay?"

"What do you want me to say?"

"That I'm wasting my time. That I should have joined college. That you've been right all along."

"Do you want me to lie?"

Taraji didn't answer.

"Come over tomorrow," Asha said. "Bring the paper."

"I'm busy."

"Doing what?"

Taraji looked at her wardrobe. The old study map still occupied three doors.

"Being disappointed."

"Fine. Bring that too."

They met at the library. Asha brought a pen that worked and a packet of biscuits; Taraji brought the test and the face of a woman attending her own sentencing.

They sorted the errors into three piles. In the first were topics Taraji had never properly learned. In the second were methods she understood until the question looked unfamiliar. In the third were careless mistakes: wrong signs, skipped instructions, hurried arithmetic.

"How many hours did you study?" Asha asked.

"Many."

"How many questions did you attempt without looking at the answer first?"

Taraji counted them. The answer was embarrassing.

For weeks she'd been studying what she liked studying. When an explanation became uncomfortable, she moved to a video that felt easier. When a question frightened her, she read the worked answer and told herself she'd known it all along.

"I'm not failing because I study at home," she said slowly.

Asha waited.

"I'm failing because I've become excellent at avoiding proof."

"You are very talented," Asha said.

Taraji laughed despite herself.

They chose one error from each pile. Taraji worked through the solutions, checking not merely the final numbers but how she'd got there. Asha left her with the questions and went to buy another pen.

For the first time, Taraji kept the score on her desk.

Not as a punishment. As an accurate description of where she was.

That night she posted a photograph of the paper to Tuesday Table.

Thirty-eight. Here are the three ways I messed it up.

Kelvin sent: Ouch.

Nuru wrote: Mine would have been worse. Which bit did you fix first?

Biko sent a voice note about tracking repeat errors instead of memorising corrections.

The conversation continued after Taraji fell asleep.

## Chapter 7: The Wrong Turn

Taraji's cousin offered her a job she couldn't afford to refuse.

It was a Saturday promotional event. She would prepare the event posters, handle registration at the door and help organise the programme. The pay was more than she usually earned in a week at Kavu Print.

There was a problem.

The group had planned a long revision afternoon that same Saturday, and Taraji was behind.

She took the job without telling them.

"One afternoon won't kill my ambitions," she told herself.

One afternoon became three days. The organiser changed the poster design five times. A printer failed. A sponsor wanted his name enlarged until it nearly touched the edge of the paper. Taraji stayed awake past midnight fixing a programme whose final version nobody seemed to have read.

On Saturday the event began late. At the door she found the organiser had promised complimentary entry to people whose names weren't on the list. She made decisions quickly, kept the queue moving and prevented a small argument between a guest and a volunteer from becoming the day's entertainment.

For two hours she felt brilliant.

Afterwards, the organiser thanked her warmly, promised to send the money and disappeared into a crowd.

On Monday he sent half.

The rest next week, he said.

Taraji stared at the message.

She had already used some of the expected money in her study budget. She had missed the revision afternoon. She was exhausted. And she had accepted the job on a handshake because she liked being the person who could solve things.

When she told Mr Otieno, he listened without interrupting.

"Did you agree on payment in writing?"

"No."

"Did you agree on when?"

"Not exactly."

"Then you have learned two expensive things."

"I did good work."

"I believe you. People can appreciate good work and still pay late."

He helped her write a firm, polite message listing what had been delivered, the agreed amount and a reasonable date for the balance. She sent it with trembling hands.

At home, Grace was cutting cloth on the dining table.

"How was the event?"

"Successful."

"Then why are you looking as though someone stole your shoes?"

Taraji told her everything, including the part where she had quietly moved money between envelopes.

Grace laid down the scissors.

"Do you want comfort or a solution?"

"Both."

"Come here, then."

They talked about work, about agreements and about the difference between earning money and having money in hand. Grace did not say I told you so, which made Taraji feel worse for approximately three minutes.

The group had sent her notes and questions from Saturday. She opened them and found a message from Nuru.

Hope the job went well. Don't vanish when things get difficult. We like you even when you're confused.

Taraji typed back:

The job went half well. Literally.

She explained what had happened.

Kelvin offered to call the organiser and "speak firmly," which everybody rejected. Biko shared a simple written agreement he'd used for small jobs. Asha asked whether Taraji wanted help catching up with the missed material.

Taraji sat with her phone in her lap.

She had chosen the job. It had not worked as she expected. She would have to deal with it.

But she didn't have to turn one bad decision into a second bad decision by hiding from the people who cared.

The organiser paid the balance eight days later. Taraji was grateful and not especially pleased with him.

She kept the payment message.

Next time, she would ask the awkward questions before she started.

## Chapter 8: A Room with No Principal

The room was above a salon.

It had four mismatched chairs, a standing fan that rattled like a tractor and a whiteboard somebody had rescued from an office clearance sale. From the window they could see the matatu stage and a shop selling phone chargers of uncertain quality.

Nuru's aunt let them use it on Sunday afternoons so long as they kept the floor clean and didn't consume the biscuits intended for salon customers.

"This," Taraji announced, standing in the doorway, "is our headquarters."

"Looks like somebody's storage room," Kelvin said.

"Good. Low overheads."

On the first Sunday eight people came. Four were from Tuesday Table. Two were Nuru's neighbours. One was a woman who had heard about the group from a library notice and wasn't sure she belonged. The eighth had arrived looking for the salon and stayed after hearing an argument about depreciation.

His name was Omondi. He drove a delivery van, was studying after work and had a daughter who believed every grown-up should know the answer to every question.

"She asked me what accountants do," he said. "I told her we make sure people don't lose track of their money."

"Not all of us are doing that very well," Taraji said.

He smiled. "It's why I'm here."

They began by putting questions on the whiteboard. Everybody could choose one. Nobody had to speak until they wanted to. Asha and Kelvin disagreed about the fastest method for solving a problem, so Biko suggested that both explain their approaches. One was quicker; the other made the reasoning easier to follow.

Both stayed on the board.

Then Nuru asked the quiet woman, who had introduced herself as Salma, whether she had a question.

Salma looked down. "I'm still at the beginning. I don't want to slow everyone."

Taraji recognised the feeling. She'd brought it home from Asha's college in her stomach, where it had sat beside her pride.

"What are you working on?" she asked.

Salma named a basic topic.

Taraji wrote it on the board. Two other people admitted they weren't comfortable with it either. In ten minutes Salma was explaining an example back to the group, her voice growing stronger with each line.

Later they ate mandazi by the window, talking about everything except accounting. Omondi described his daughter's insistence that he attend a school event in a shirt with no wrinkles. Nuru had a story about a client who fell asleep while having her hair braided. Kelvin confessed he once travelled all the way to college on a public holiday.

"I was very early," he said.

"By twenty-four hours," Asha replied.

Taraji laughed until she couldn't breathe.

At the end, several people asked whether the meetings would continue.

"Of course," Taraji said.

Nuru gave her a look.

"What's the cost?" Omondi asked.

"Nothing," Taraji said, then glanced at the lights and fan. "We might need to contribute something for electricity, if we use the room regularly. Nobody should be turned away because they can't."

"Then we should ask Auntie first," Nuru said. "And we shouldn't collect money before we know what we're paying for."

Taraji nodded. She was learning.

As the room emptied, Biko lingered at the whiteboard.

"You should know," he said, "you did something good here."

"We solved four questions and stole a room."

"You made it easy for people to admit they didn't know."

Taraji considered that.

All week she had been worrying that she wasn't managing her education properly because it didn't resemble a college.

Now she was standing in a room with a cracked window, a restless fan and a floor smelling faintly of hair products, looking at the evidence of people learning together.

It didn't resemble a college.

That, she thought, was not necessarily a problem.
