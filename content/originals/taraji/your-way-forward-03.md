## Chapter 9: The People Who Turn Up

On the third Sunday, Nuru did not come.

At first nobody worried. The salon was busy. Her aunt frequently needed her help. Nuru's voice notes arrived at strange hours, sometimes interrupted by water running into a basin or a client asking if the braids were almost finished.

By five, though, there was no message.

Taraji called. No answer.

She was halfway through composing a dramatic announcement about the disappearance of their founding member when Nuru finally replied.

At home. Mum isn't well. Can't come.

Taraji typed:

Are you okay?

The message sat unread for nearly an hour.

On Monday she visited after work. She brought oranges because she remembered her mother always brought oranges when somebody was sick, and then stood outside the door wondering whether oranges were an appropriate gift for every illness.

Nuru opened it wearing slippers and an expression of profound fatigue.

"You've brought fruit," she said.

"I panicked."

"Come in."

Nuru's mother was resting in the other room. Her condition was being assessed at a clinic, and the household had become a careful arrangement of errands, meals and people taking turns to stay nearby. Nuru had been doing much of the arranging.

"I haven't opened a book in five days," she said, as Taraji helped wash cups.

"So don't open one today."

"You've suddenly become very relaxed about studying."

"I have a range of personalities."

Nuru managed a smile.

They sat on the doorstep while evening came down around the neighbourhood. For once Taraji did not fill every silence.

"I thought you came to convince me not to drop out of the group," Nuru said eventually.

"I came because you're my friend."

Nuru looked away.

Then she said, "Good. Because I might need a little time."

At Tuesday Table, Taraji explained only what Nuru had agreed she could share. Asha offered notes. Biko offered to check one difficult question by phone when Nuru felt ready. Omondi asked whether a practical errand would help. Nobody insisted she keep up with the group's schedule.

Kelvin, after some deliberation, sent a message.

Tell Nuru the fan is behaving better without her.

Taraji read it twice before replying:

That is the worst comforting message ever written.

He sent another.

Tell her we miss her.

This one she forwarded.

The group itself had problems. More people wanted to join than the salon room could hold comfortably. Some came once and never returned. Two participants argued about who was doing more work. Someone forwarded an advertisement promising miraculous exam results, and Taraji spent a miserable afternoon explaining why it shouldn't remain in the chat.

She had wanted a community. She hadn't realised it would require so much listening.

One Sunday, when only four people arrived, she sat staring at the empty chairs.

"Maybe it's failing," she told Biko.

"By what measure?"

"We had eight. Now there are four."

"Last week Salma told me she finally understood the topic she was afraid to ask about. Nuru is going through a difficult time and knows people are thinking of her. Omondi brought his daughter here to wait for him, and she now calls this place his school."

"Your point?"

"You're counting chairs."

Taraji turned to look at him.

He returned to his notebook, apparently finished with the conversation.

She stayed for the next hour, helping Kelvin revise a method he'd previously refused to admit he didn't understand.

The room felt a little less empty.

## Chapter 10: The Deadline

Official deadlines have no interest in your personal philosophy.

Taraji discovered this while trying to complete her examination arrangements on a borrowed laptop at the library.

A page refused to load. She refreshed it. Her internet bundle expired. She bought another. Then she found a detail she needed to verify before continuing.

"Of course," she muttered. "The one document I put somewhere safe."

Mrs Wairimu looked up from behind her desk.

"Never put anything somewhere safe," she said. "Put it somewhere you can find."

Taraji located the document that evening beneath a stack of old printing samples.

She had been telling herself for weeks that she would deal with the examination arrangements when she was ready. But readiness, she now understood, did not move published dates. She checked the examining body's official instructions, confirmed what applied to her and wrote down the remaining steps.

No cousin's friend. No forwarded screenshot presented as fact. No guessing.

Asha helped her check the list while they walked home from the market.

"You are becoming responsible," Asha said.

"Please don't tell anybody. I have a reputation."

Taraji had enough money set aside for the examination-related expenses she had planned. Not as much spare money as she'd hoped. The event job had taught her to stop treating promises as cash. The new phone she wanted would have to wait.

That night Grace found her checking every detail again.

"Are you frightened?" she asked.

"A little."

"Of the exam?"

"Of discovering that I was wrong about everything."

Grace pulled out the chair beside her.

"When you were small, you insisted on walking to the shop alone. You took a wrong turn, found yourself at the football field and came home furious with the whole neighbourhood."

"I was seven."

"You blamed the road."

"The road was misleading."

Grace smiled.

"Do you remember what happened the next day?"

"I went again."

"You asked where to turn."

Taraji nodded.

"You have never been afraid of choosing," Grace said. "Sometimes you have been afraid of asking."

Taraji turned the laptop towards her and showed her the confirmation she had received for the step she had completed.

Grace read it carefully.

"Good," she said. "Now go to bed."

Taraji saved the information in two places, put the relevant date in her calendar and sent a message to Tuesday Table reminding everyone to check their own official arrangements. She did not assume the same requirements or deadlines applied to all of them.

Then she went to bed.

She was surprised to discover that sleeping was more useful than worrying.

The next morning she attempted another timed practice paper. Her score was better than thirty-eight. Not spectacular. Better.

More importantly, when she reviewed the errors, she knew exactly which ones she needed to work on.

## Chapter 11: A Very Ordinary Examination Morning

The examination morning began with a missing sock.

Taraji found it inside Lenny's schoolbag. Nobody could explain how it had got there, least of all Lenny, who insisted he was being falsely accused of a sock-related crime.

She ate breakfast while Grace checked that she had what she needed.

"Have you got—"

"Yes."

"And the—"

"Yes."

"Did you check—"

"Mum."

Grace held up both hands. "Fine. You are in charge."

Taraji had chosen to travel early enough to allow for delays. That decision saved her when the first matatu she boarded stopped for an argument about change and stayed stopped long enough for two passengers to begin discussing politics.

She got out, found another and arrived with time to spare.

At the examination centre she saw students clustered in pairs. Some whispered formulas. Some stared into space. A young man in a blazer was explaining something to nobody in particular. Everyone looked simultaneously grown-up and terrified.

Taraji checked the instructions and joined the appropriate line.

Her phone buzzed one last time before she put it away.

Nuru: Don't be clever. Read the question.

Asha: You know more than you think. But still read the question.

Kelvin: If there's depreciation, be respectful.

Biko: One step at a time.

Omondi had sent a voice note of his daughter shouting, "Good luck, Auntie Taraji!" so loudly that Taraji laughed in the queue.

Then the doors opened.

Inside, the room had the peculiar quiet of people who had been taught to fear the sound of their own breathing. Taraji found her seat, placed her permitted materials where instructed and read the paper.

The first question was familiar.

So familiar that she nearly rushed.

She heard Nuru's voice in her head. Don't be clever. Read the question.

The second problem was harder. She began one method, recognised an error and corrected it. For a moment she felt the old panic: the urge to look for some hidden trick, some perfect shortcut that would prove she belonged there.

Instead she drew a line, wrote what she knew and worked from the information in front of her.

There were questions she could answer well, questions she could answer partly and one that resisted her for too long. She moved on and returned when she had time.

She wasn't suddenly brilliant. She wasn't suddenly free of doubt.

She was prepared enough to keep thinking.

When the invigilator announced the end, Taraji put down her pen.

Outside, the sun was high and the street sounded offensively normal. Vendors called out to customers. Cars hooted. Somebody laughed into a phone.

Taraji stood for a moment, adjusting to the fact that a day she'd spent months thinking about had become an ordinary afternoon.

Asha called.

"How was it?"

"I answered things."

"Very informative."

"Some of them correctly, I hope."

"Are you hungry?"

"Absolutely."

They met for lunch. Taraji refused to spend the meal checking every answer against memory, which was difficult because she remembered several of them at inconvenient moments. Asha talked about a lecturer who'd lost his glasses on top of his own head. Taraji told her about the sock.

They ate and laughed and allowed the paper to be finished.

On the way home, Taraji bought tomatoes.

This time she remembered the onions without being asked.

## Chapter 12: The Door She Opened

The results came on a Thursday.

Taraji saw the notification while counting the change a customer had given her at Kavu Print. She checked the figure, handed over the receipt and put the phone beneath the counter.

Mr Otieno watched her.

"Something important?"

"Maybe."

"Then you can look after this customer leaves."

The customer left.

Taraji remained motionless.

Mr Otieno folded his arms. "You are the loudest person I employ. Why are you suddenly quiet?"

She picked up the phone.

The result showed she had passed the paper she had taken.

For a second she didn't understand what she was seeing. Then she read it again, carefully. There was no music. No confetti. No voice announcing that her entire future was now secure.

Just the result of one examination.

She sat on the stool behind the counter.

Mr Otieno leaned over. "Well?"

"I passed."

"Good."

"That's all you've got?"

He placed a fresh order slip in front of her.

"I also have forty invitations that need cutting."

She laughed, and then, unexpectedly, cried.

He pretended not to notice. Five minutes later he bought her a soda.

At home Grace checked the result herself. Lenny asked whether Taraji was now rich. Asha screamed over the phone. Nuru sent three voice notes, each louder than the previous one. Kelvin claimed he had predicted this outcome, although nobody could recall him saying so.

Biko wrote:

Well earned. What's next?

Taraji looked at the message for a long time.

She had passed one paper. She had not completed the whole qualification. Her part-time work was still irregular. There were other examinations ahead, other costs, other decisions. She would make more mistakes. Some might be bigger than the ones she had already made.

She knew all that.

But something had changed.

She no longer needed to convince everybody that her way could work. She had evidence that she could learn, adapt and meet a real standard without following the schedule somebody else had chosen for her.

And she no longer mistook independence for isolation.

On Sunday she returned to the room above the salon.

Nuru was there, her mother doing better, with a bag of mandazi she'd brought from home. Omondi was helping his daughter write her name in big letters on scrap paper. Asha had come with two classmates. Kelvin was trying to repair the standing fan using instructions from a video, while Biko kept reminding him that he was not qualified to repair electrical appliances.

"Turn it off first!" everybody shouted.

Kelvin raised his hands and stepped away.

Taraji laughed.

A young woman stood at the doorway holding a notebook.

"Excuse me," she said. "Is this the CPA group?"

"Sometimes," Nuru replied. "Other times it's a comedy club."

The newcomer smiled nervously.

"I heard I could come even if I'm not in college."

Taraji moved a chair away from the wall.

"What's your name?"

"Imani."

"Come in, Imani."

Imani stepped inside and looked around.

"Who teaches here?" she asked.

Taraji glanced at the whiteboard. Asha was explaining something to Salma. Biko had started a quiet conversation with Omondi. Nuru was offering tea to the newcomer. Even Kelvin, deprived of his repair project, was helping find an extra chair.

"Different people, on different days," Taraji said.

"But who's in charge?"

Taraji considered giving a magnificent answer. She was very good at magnificent answers. She could have talked about freedom and courage, about building a future nobody had mapped for you, about the stubborn beauty of beginning with what you have.

Instead she pulled out a chair.

"Tell us what you're trying to learn."

Imani sat.

Outside, matatus called for passengers. Downstairs, somebody switched on the salon radio. The fan made one heroic rattling noise before Biko unplugged it for good.

Taraji opened her notebook.

There was a new question on the board.

She was not sure of the answer.

She picked up her pen anyway.

The end.
