# Welcome to Your CPA Student Life

VibeSchool Originals · An independent learner's companion · English · Text-only edition

## A chair is enough to begin

Nobody will take attendance when you open this book.

You might be reading it between customers at work. You might be on a matatu, waiting for a phone to charge, or sitting at the one table in the house where everybody puts their keys. Perhaps you've already registered for CPA examinations. Perhaps you're still trying to work out what becoming an accountant will demand of you.

Either way, welcome.

You are not required to look like a college student to become a serious student. There is no uniform for curiosity. There is no particular building in which understanding takes place.

There are, however, real things to learn and real standards to meet. The freedom to study independently does not make the work disappear. It gives you the opportunity—and the responsibility—to decide how you will do it.

This small book isn't here to supervise you. It won't announce that successful people wake up at five, that every evening belongs to revision, or that distractions can be defeated with one beautiful timetable.

It is here to keep you company as you make some useful decisions.

Start wherever you are.

## You get to choose what a student looks like

Picture two people preparing for the same professional examination.

One is walking across a college compound, greeting friends and looking for a classroom. The other has finished an afternoon shift and is sitting at home with a calculator and a borrowed laptop.

The first is not automatically more committed. The second is not automatically more independent. Both will need to understand the material. Both will have to demonstrate what they know under the examination rules.

What differs is how they organise the days around that work.

Perhaps you learn best through conversation. Perhaps you prefer reading before anyone speaks. Perhaps a timetable calms you. Perhaps your work shifts change every week, so a fixed timetable becomes a source of frustration.

You are allowed to find out.

Try a method long enough to judge it. Ask whether you're actually learning. Keep what helps. Change what doesn't.

A useful question is not, "Am I studying the correct way?"

It is, "What can I understand and do now that I could not understand or do before?"

## The qualification is real. So is your responsibility.

Independent learning gives you freedom over your preparation, not freedom to invent examination requirements.

Find the current official information from the examining body. Understand which qualification you are pursuing, which level and papers apply to you, what registration involves, what it costs, where and how examinations are conducted, and which deadlines matter.

For CPA in Kenya, check KASNEB's current official guidance rather than depending on an old screenshot or somebody else's memory. Requirements, dates and paper arrangements can change. A helpful friend is not a substitute for the official instructions.

Keep your own record of what you've confirmed and where you confirmed it. Save important documents in a place you can find again. Don't pay an unfamiliar intermediary simply because they sound confident on the phone.

There is nothing glamorous about this part of student life.

It is still part of your future.

## The day a topic refuses to make sense

Sooner or later you will meet a question that doesn't care how motivated you are.

You will read it three times. You may understand the words separately and still have no idea what to do with them together.

That moment doesn't tell you whether you belong in accounting. It tells you that you've reached the edge of what you currently know.

Try identifying the exact place where you lose the thread. Is it the meaning of a term? The reason for an adjustment? A calculation? The way a question has been phrased?

Look at an explanation or worked example, then close it and attempt a similar problem yourself. If your answer is wrong, find out why. A correct answer that you copied is not the same as a method you can use when the numbers change.

Ask for help when you need it. A classmate, a study partner, a suitable teacher, a reliable learning resource or a professional who is willing to explain may be able to show you what you missed.

You haven't surrendered your independence by asking.

You've used it well.

## Nobody can give you all your hours back

Time is different when you're studying without a college timetable.

Nobody will ring a bell when the lesson begins. Nobody will ask where you were yesterday. Some days that's wonderful. You can work, care for family, rest or study when your mind is clear.

Other days the freedom stretches until there is no studying left in it.

You don't need to punish yourself with a schedule that ignores your life.

Look at the week you actually have. Count your working hours, responsibilities, travel, sleep and ordinary interruptions. Decide what you can reasonably give your studies. Begin with something you can keep doing.

If a plan fails on Tuesday, don't spend the rest of the week announcing that you're a failure. Find the reason. Change the plan if necessary. Return to the work.

A missed session is an event, not an identity.

And rest is not a crime committed against ambition.

## The people part

A college often supplies company without anyone having to arrange it. You sit beside someone in class, meet again at lunch, complain about the same difficult question and, one day, realise you're friends.

When you study independently, you may have to make the first move.

That might be as simple as asking somebody what paper they're studying. You might join a moderated study discussion, meet a friend at a public library, or build a small group around questions you all want to understand.

You don't have to enjoy large groups. One reliable study partner can be enough. You don't have to perform confidence for strangers. You can listen first.

Choose people who respect your time and boundaries. Don't feel obliged to share your home address, private documents, examination credentials or personal contact details to prove that you belong.

A good student community does not demand that you all learn alike. It creates room for people who don't.

And remember that friendships need moments when nobody is talking about examinations. Eat together. Laugh. Celebrate birthdays. Ask how someone's family is doing. Be a person among people.

## Money is part of the story

Studying independently may reduce certain costs, especially regular commuting or college tuition. It will not make every expense vanish.

A reliable device, data, electricity, learning materials and examination arrangements may all cost money. Some students support themselves through work or business. Others share household resources. Some have access to a quiet space; others must negotiate for one.

Make your plans with the money that is available, not the money somebody has promised.

When you can, use free or low-cost legitimate resources and public study spaces. Ask what you genuinely need before paying for something advertised as essential. A more expensive notebook won't teach a topic by itself.

Don't compare your circumstances with a beautifully arranged desk in a photograph. You may be looking at somebody's best day.

Your task is to build a learning life you can sustain.

## Your first seven days

There is no ceremony you must attend before beginning. You can give yourself a starting point.

On your first day, write down why you want to pursue CPA. Not the version that sounds impressive at a family gathering. The reason you come back to when you feel tired.

On another day, check the official route you need to follow. Learn the requirements that apply to you and note what you still need to confirm.

Find one reliable learning resource and attempt a small piece of actual work. Notice where you get stuck.

Look at the coming week and protect a realistic amount of study time. Leave space for the unexpected.

Introduce yourself to one person who might understand the journey. You are not applying for friendship. You're opening a conversation.

At the end of the week, look at what happened. What worked? What didn't? Did you learn something? What would you like to try differently?

There is no need to complete these steps in a particular order, or to pretend that a difficult week has been easy.

You only need a beginning that belongs to you.

## Before you go

In Your Way Forward, Taraji chooses to prepare for CPA independently. She is outgoing, determined and very capable of turning a small problem into an interesting one. She makes decisions quickly, sometimes too quickly. She learns that freedom brings consequences and that refusing a conventional route does not require refusing other people.

Taraji is fictional. Her mistakes and friendships belong to her story. Your choices will belong to yours.

Maybe you'll attend college later. Maybe you won't. Maybe your career will take an unexpected turn. Choosing your own way doesn't mean promising never to change your mind.

It means paying attention to who you are, what you're trying to do and what the evidence of your life is telling you.

You can begin with an imperfect plan.

You can ask for help.

You can make a decision, face what follows and choose again.

And wherever you study, there are other people trying to build a future too.

You may find some of them along the way.
