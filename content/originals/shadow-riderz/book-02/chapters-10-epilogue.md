## Chapter Ten: The Hearing at Shabe Hall

By eight o'clock the next morning, Shabe Community Hall was full.

There were mechanics from Kwa Kariz.

Shopkeepers from Wakibe.

Families from Tamtam.

Children from Langa.

Volunteers from Bonde.

And a surprising number of people who had come simply because they wanted to know whether Garage Zero was really haunted.

Mama Sia arrived carrying a stack of books.

Mdavido stared.

"Are those for the hearing?"

"For anybody who gets bored," she said.

He nodded approvingly.

"Excellent planning."

At the front sat a community officer, a land-records clerk and the building inspector.

Reva, Biko and Kariz had brought the archived agreement copy from the blue box.

Mr. Barasa had helped them identify the public record reference.

Mrs. Ndeda, the retired lawyer, had also provided a letter confirming that she had prepared the original community workshop documents.

The clerk reviewed the documents and explained that they corresponded to an old community-use entry. The full file still needed formal reconciliation, but the original workshop could not simply be sold as abandoned property on the strength of one agent's letter.

Reva felt her shoulders relax.

Then Mr. Mavua stood.

"With respect," he said smoothly, "the site has been dangerous for years. Its future must be decided responsibly."

"Agreed," the building inspector replied. "The damaged structure remains closed until repaired and inspected. That is separate from ownership."

Mavua paused.

"Naturally."

Phylmyk watched him.

He had arrived with two assistants and a large briefcase.

One assistant kept looking toward the exit.

He was a thin man wearing a pale jacket.

Sefu leaned toward Phylmyk.

"That looks like the man in the photograph."

"Are you certain?"

"Not certain. But look at the jacket."

Phylmyk showed the photograph to the safety officer rather than confronting him.

The officer walked over to the assistant.

They spoke quietly.

The man looked at the photograph.

His expression changed.

A few minutes later, he agreed to give a statement outside the hall.

The hearing continued.

Reva explained how Garage Zero had helped the community during the floods.

Biko described Zero One, the old delivery tricycle.

Zuri showed photographs of the safe repair shed and the test completed under adult supervision.

Then a parent from Bonde stood.

"My daughter used the books Mama Sia delivered when our road was flooded. I remember that tricycle."

Another person spoke.

"I remember it too. It carried water and school materials."

Soon people were sharing memories.

Not ghost stories.

Real stories.

The hearing began to feel less like a dispute over empty land and more like a town remembering something it had almost forgotten.

Then the safety officer returned.

He spoke briefly to the chairperson.

The chairperson paused proceedings and asked everyone to remain calm.

"We have received information about the report alleging stolen workshop tools," she announced. "That allegation has not been substantiated. The office will correct its records and investigate how the report was submitted."

Zuri closed her eyes.

Reva reached for her hand.

The chairperson continued.

"We have also received new information about equipment removed from the damaged Garage Zero building. That will be addressed separately. No decision about the site's future will be based on rumours."

Mr. Mavua looked at the ceiling.

For the first time, his smile had disappeared.

Labonator leaned toward Dantez.

"Something tells me he didn't enjoy that."

Dantez almost smiled.

"Probably."

The chairperson asked Reva one final question.

"What do you propose for Garage Zero?"

Reva stood.

"Repair the building if it can be made safe. Preserve the old records. Let the community decide how the site should serve people again."

She looked at Zuri.

"And allow the next generation to learn without being laughed out of the workshop."

Zuri sat very straight.

Dantez noticed her hands trembling.

He leaned toward her.

"You did well."

She whispered back, "I haven't said anything."

"You made the machine. That counts."

At the front, the chairperson consulted the other officials.

Then she announced that the proposed sale and clearance would not proceed on the current incomplete ownership file. The council would verify the records and assess what repairs were needed before any new use was approved.

The old garage remained closed.

The safe repair shed could continue only under Reva's supervision and the conditions already approved for it.

It wasn't a magical victory.

But it was a real one.

Zuri smiled.

Then the doors at the back of the hall opened.

The man in the pale jacket returned with the safety officer.

And he was carrying a familiar blue metal box.

---

## Chapter Eleven: The Box That Wasn't Supposed to Leave

The man introduced himself as Tano.

He worked as a contractor on property inspections.

The box had been taken from the damaged office at Garage Zero.

He placed it on a table with the safety officer present.

Reva recognised it at once.

"That is our old workshop archive box."

Biko nodded.

"We used to keep duplicate agreements in it."

"Why did you take it?" Kariz asked.

Tano looked down.

"I was asked to photograph what was inside the building before the property assessment."

"By whom?"

"The agency working on the proposed sale."

"Did you have permission to enter?" the officer asked.

Tano hesitated.

"No."

The hall went very quiet.

He continued.

"I found some papers and thought the box might contain documents relevant to the property. I should have reported it properly. Instead I took it to my vehicle."

Phylmyk stared at him.

"Did you write the notes telling people Garage Zero was open?"

Tano shook his head.

"Not those first notes."

"Did you send the message about the blue box?"

He swallowed.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I thought somebody had moved the original papers from the garage into the tricycle. I wanted to know where they were."

Dantez folded his arms.

"So you frightened a child to find records."

Tano looked ashamed.

"I didn't think it would become this serious."

Reva's voice was quiet.

"It was serious the moment you entered without permission and made accusations."

The officer recorded his statement. The property office would review the contractor's conduct and the misleading complaints. The contents of the box would be checked and returned to their proper custodian after the relevant procedures.

Phylmyk looked toward Mr. Mavua.

He was speaking urgently to another official.

She wanted to march over and demand answers.

Yobra gently touched her shoulder.

"Evidence first."

She nodded.

Tano looked at the children again.

"There is one more thing."

"What?" Dantez asked.

"The blue light and the radio."

Everybody leaned closer.

"I was inside at night once, before I realised how unsafe the structure was. I used an old two-way radio to speak to my supervisor outside."

Phylmyk remembered the first story.

"Route Three, can you hear me?"

Tano stared.

"Yes. Those were my words."

"Then somebody answered, 'Leave the blue box where it is.'"

He nodded.

"That's right."

Labonator looked toward Dantez.

"So the phantom was a contractor with a radio?"

"Apparently."

Tano went on.

"After I left, I put an old recording device near the workshop radio to make it sound as though somebody was still speaking there. I thought it would keep curious people away."

Phylmyk's mouth fell open.

"You created a ghost story!"

"I shouldn't have."

"No," Reva said firmly. "You shouldn't."

"And the bell?" Mdavido asked.

"The loose metal plate was already striking the old bell bracket in the wind," Tano replied. "I didn't arrange that."

Vanny Boy adjusted his glasses.

"An unintended combination of natural acoustics and deliberate audio playback created the appearance of unexplained activity."

Mdavido smiled.

"A windy bell and a fake recording."

"Correct."

Phylmyk looked at the blue box.

The mystery finally had a shape.

Zuri had made the shadows.

The old timer had made the blue light.

The wind had made the bell ring.

Tano had made the mysterious voice.

And the rumours had turned ordinary events into a ghost story that frightened people away from the workshop.

But one question remained.

"Who wrote the note about the tricycle proving the land belonged to Gema?" Phylmyk asked.

Sefu slowly raised his hand.

Everyone turned.

"I did."

Zuri stared at her brother.

"YOU?"

He looked embarrassed.

"I thought if Dantez saw how important Zero One was, he would help you. So I wrote something dramatic and left it at Wakibe."

Labonator laughed.

"Congratulations. You started a town-wide mystery."

Sefu covered his face.

"I'm sorry."

Zuri looked at him for a long moment.

Then sighed.

"You could have asked me."

"I did. You said you didn't need help."

"I didn't want people laughing at my work."

"Well, now they're not laughing."

Zuri looked toward the adults discussing the old records.

She smiled faintly.

"No. They're listening."

At that moment, thunder rolled over Shabe.

Rain began hammering the roof.

A teacher hurried into the hall.

"The lower road is flooding. People need to use the safe route home."

Everyone turned toward the windows.

The rain was growing heavier.

And the road outside was filling with water.

For the first time in years, Gema needed a good delivery plan again.

---

## Chapter Twelve: A Workshop for Tomorrow

The rain lasted two hours.

The lower road near Shabe became impassable, and the authorities directed people to higher, safer routes.

Nobody went through the flooded section.

The community hall opened a sheltered waiting area.

Wadani brought tea.

Mama Sia organised books for the children.

Kariz and other adults helped move dry supplies from the storage room into the hall.

Dantez offered to help maintain a simple list of what had been received and where it was going. The organisers approved it, and no personal information was shared beyond what was needed for the task.

Vanny checked the quantities.

Mdavido read labels.

Amani helped arrange donations by type.

Labonator carried boxes.

Lucanji carried three small packets of biscuits.

Nobody had assigned him that job.

"Lucanji," Phylmyk asked, "are those for the waiting families?"

He stopped.

"Yes."

"Then why are two open?"

He considered.

"Quality inspection."

Mdavido burst out laughing.

Across the hall, Zuri stood beside Zero One.

The tricycle was parked in the approved sheltered yard.

It had passed its supervised test, but Reva would not allow it onto public roads or near floodwater.

Zuri understood.

"Could we use it here?" she asked.

Kariz inspected the dry, fenced path between the hall's storage room and the covered entrance.

"For a small demonstration with the mechanic supervising, yes. Not on the road."

Zuri smiled.

They placed a few empty crates on the cargo platform.

She guided Zero One along the short level yard.

Slowly.

Carefully.

The old wheels turned.

The machine moved.

Labonator cheered as though she had won the World Cup.

"ZERO ONE!"

The adults clapped.

Zuri brought the tricycle to a stop and stepped off.

She looked at the crowd.

Then at the old photograph of her mother beside the original machine.

For a moment, she couldn't speak.

Reva wrapped an arm around her.

"You've brought it back."

Zuri shook her head.

"Not alone."

She looked at Dantez.

At Biko.

At Kariz.

At Labonator.

At Sefu.

And at the children who had helped carry the records to the public hearing.

"Everybody helped."

Phylmyk smiled.

"That's what the original riders did."

By evening, the rain had stopped.

The roads were checked before people went home.

Garage Zero's damaged building remained closed, but the ownership records were being reconciled and the community had agreed to explore a safe repair-training project when the necessary approvals and repairs were complete.

There would be no private sale based on the misleading documents.

There would be no ghost stories used to frighten children away.

And Zuri would not have to pretend her work was something to be ashamed of.

At Kwa Kariz, Kariz placed a new sign beside the old volunteer photographs.

**GARAGE ZERO — THE FIRST WORKSHOP**

**THREE ROUTES. FIVE RIDERS. ONE GEMA.**

Beneath it was a small photograph of Zuri and Zero One.

Dantez stood looking at it.

Labonator approached.

"Thinking about buying a tricycle?"

"No."

"Building one?"

"Maybe."

"Painting it blue?"

"Definitely not."

Labonator put a hand over his heart.

"You wound me."

Dantez smiled.

Then his phone rang.

A message from Zuri.

**Thank you for taking my work seriously.**

He read it twice.

Then typed:

**Next time, show everyone what you built. It's worth seeing.**

He put away the phone.

Phylmyk joined him.

"How does it feel?"

"What?"

"Solving a mystery without stealing, lying or pretending to be the smartest person in Gema."

Dantez considered.

"Surprisingly good."

She smiled.

"Progress."

Labonator lifted his football.

"Now that the mystery is over, can we please return to important things?"

Phylmyk folded her arms.

"Like what?"

"Football."

Mdavido shouted from across the yard.

"YOU MEAN KICKING BALLS INTO TOMATO STALLS?"

Everyone burst out laughing.

Labonator pointed at him.

"YOU WILL NEVER FORGET THAT, WILL YOU?"

"NEVER!"

And for a moment, Gema sounded exactly as it should.

Loud.

Busy.

Full of arguments.

Full of friends.

And completely alive.

## Epilogue: The Fourth Road

Late that night, Biko sat alone in his workshop.

He had been sorting old photographs from the flood years.

There were pictures of the five riders.

Pictures of damaged bridges.

Pictures of books being carried into Tamtam.

Then he found one photograph he did not remember taking.

It showed the original signal shed near Shabe.

The shed was small, barely larger than a kiosk.

On the door was a painted symbol.

Three curved roads.

Five dots.

But beneath it somebody had added another line.

A fourth road.

Biko frowned.

He turned the picture over.

A message was written in pencil.

**THE FOURTH ROAD WAS NEVER ON THE MAP.**

He stared at the words.

Then his old radio crackled.

*Hiss.*

Biko froze.

The radio had been disconnected from its charging cable.

Its battery indicator still showed power.

A voice came through.

"Route Four."

Biko leaned forward.

"Who is this?"

Static.

Then:

"Route Four. Delivery completed."

Biko's hands trembled.

He reached for the receiver.

"Who is speaking?"

The voice paused.

Then said:

"Ask Kito about the night the bridge disappeared."

The transmission ended.

Biko sat motionless.

Outside, Gema was quiet.

Far away, a dog barked.

He looked down at the photograph again.

Five riders.

Three roads.

A fourth road that nobody had ever mentioned.

He slowly reached for his phone.

And called Kariz.

"Kariz?"

"Yes?"

"I think we missed something four years ago."

A pause.

"What?"

Biko stared at the old photograph.

"The map."

"What about it?"

Biko swallowed.

"There were never only three routes."
