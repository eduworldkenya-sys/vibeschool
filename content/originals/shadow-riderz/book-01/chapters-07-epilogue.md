## Chapter Seven: A Voice from Four Years Ago

Mara's words remained in Dantez's head all afternoon.

*The recording was meant for you.*

Why him?

He wasn't one of the original riders. Four years ago, he had been a child who spent most of his time taking apart broken radios and getting told off for leaving screws on the sitting-room floor.

He had never met Biko.

At Kwa Kariz, Kariz listened to the story carefully. The safety officer took the details of the messages and the missing memory card.

Then Nia made a call to her father.

"Mzee Kito? It's me. We're with Kariz."

She listened.

"Yes, the garage."

More listening.

Then her eyebrows rose.

"You're serious?"

She ended the call.

"He's coming here," she said.

Dantez looked toward the gate.

"With Biko?"

"Possibly."

Labonator took a long breath.

"If one more mysterious person comes to this garage, I'm charging an entrance fee."

Kariz looked at him.

"You're not charging anyone."

"Just an idea."

"Think of another."

Mdavido, who was reading outside, called through the window.

"Charge for parking. Your blue car takes up half the yard!"

"MY BLUE CAR IS A WORK OF ART!"

"Then put it in a museum!"

Dantez laughed despite himself.

An hour later, a small white pickup entered the garage.

Mzee Kito climbed out first.

He was broad-shouldered, with grey hair and hands rough from years of repairing bicycles. He wore a faded rain jacket and carried the original Three-Five badge in a small wooden box.

A second man followed.

He was thin, with a short beard and kind, tired eyes.

"Biko," Kariz said.

The thin man smiled.

"Kariz."

They shook hands.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Then Kariz said, "You disappeared."

Biko nodded.

"I did."

"Four years."

"I know."

"Not even a letter."

Biko looked down.

"I was ashamed."

The words surprised Dantez.

Ashamed of what?

Biko sat on a stool.

"After the flood, I recorded a message on an old recorder. I wanted the five of us to remember why we started helping. When the supplies disappeared, we argued. Some of us accused the others. I couldn't stand it."

Kariz nodded slowly.

"I remember."

"I left Gema to work in Nakuru. Later I heard the Shadow Riderz name was being used in a technology campaign. Then somebody showed me that black helmet."

Dantez glanced at the workbench.

"You came to the Tomorrow Holdings office."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"To warn them that the badge and the group's history belonged to the volunteers. I also left a copy of my old recording. I thought it might remind whoever was using our name what it stood for."

"What happened to the recording?" Phylmyk asked.

Biko looked at Mara, who had come with the adults.

"I put it in the helmet pocket because she agreed to pass it along. It was a foolish method, and I'm sorry."

Mara nodded.

"But I didn't take it out," she said. "When the helmet reached Kwa Kariz, the pocket was empty."

Biko looked toward the garage gate.

"Then perhaps somebody intercepted it."

Kariz folded his arms.

"Who?"

A voice came from the doorway.

"I might know."

Everybody turned.

Wadani.

She held an old brown envelope in one hand.

"Before anyone accuses anyone," she said, "please let me explain."

Phylmyk straightened.

"Wadani?"

Wadani walked to the counter.

"Two days ago, I found this envelope beside Wakibe's delivery shelf. It was addressed to the Gema relief volunteers. Inside was a memory card and a note asking me to give it to Kariz."

Dantez blinked.

"Why didn't you?"

"I did. Yesterday."

Kariz stared at her.

"You gave me an envelope with old photographs and a memory card. I put it in the office safe for the officer."

Everyone looked at Kariz.

He scratched his head.

"There's been a lot happening in my garage!"

Labonator threw up his hands.

"HOW CAN EVERYBODY KEEP FORGETTING IMPORTANT THINGS?"

Mdavido leaned through the window.

"Because this town needs a filing cabinet the size of a mountain."

The officer checked the envelope, recorded its handover, and with Biko's permission arranged for the audio to be played through an ordinary speaker.

No secret passwords.

No stolen accounts.

Just an old recording from an old volunteer.

The speaker crackled.

A younger voice came through.

It was Biko.

*Testing. Testing. Gema Three-Five recording, final day of relief work.*

A burst of laughter sounded in the background.

Then another voice said:

*Biko, stop recording and help carry these boxes!*

Everyone chuckled.

The recording continued.

*We started with five riders and three roads. We had no money, no special uniforms, and no promise of prizes. But we had neighbours who needed help.*

Dantez listened.

*If anyone ever uses the Shadow Riderz name again, let it be for helping people, not frightening them.*

Biko's younger voice paused.

*And if the next generation builds something better than motorcycles and paper maps, let them use it to serve Gema.*

The recording ended.

For a long moment, nobody spoke.

Dantez looked at the helmet.

The fake logo.

The threats.

The invitations.

The promises of power.

He felt embarrassed.

He had thought being chosen meant being important.

But the original volunteers had never needed anyone to choose them.

They had simply seen a problem and helped.

Labonator touched his shoulder.

"Are you okay?"

Dantez nodded slowly.

"Yes."

Then he looked at Biko.

"Why was the recording meant for me?"

Biko smiled.

"Because I heard you built a system to track deliveries. You called it Guardian Grid 35."

Dantez looked down.

"Yes."

"And then you used it the wrong way."

Dantez nodded.

"Yes."

Biko leaned closer.

"Young man, inventing something clever is not the same as doing something good."

Dantez said nothing.

"But you can still choose to do good with it."

For once, Dantez didn't argue.

## Chapter Eight: The Real Shadow Riderz

Three days later, Kwa Kariz hosted the strangest gathering in its history.

Five old volunteers sat at a table made from two wooden doors resting on oil drums.

Kariz had cleaned the yard.

Wadani brought tea and mandazi.

Mama Sia brought books.

The children arrived from Langa, Shabe, Tamtam and Bonde.

And Labonator had repaired his safety reminder machine.

"Please," Dantez begged, "don't switch it on."

Labonator grinned.

"It's improved."

"Those are the words you said before it fell off the table."

"This time it has a proper stand."

He pressed the button.

A soft voice said:

**CHECK YOUR VEHICLE BEFORE YOU TRAVEL.**

Kariz looked impressed.

"That's actually useful."

Labonator straightened proudly.

Then the machine added:

**AND NEVER LET LABONATOR DRIVE WITHOUT SUPERVISION.**

Everyone burst out laughing.

Labonator stared at Dantez.

"You changed the recording!"

Dantez grinned.

"Engineering."

"YOU!"

The two boys chased each other around the workshop until Kariz ordered them to sit.

Then Mzee Kito stood.

"We came here because the children have discovered something important," he said.

The others went quiet.

"The Shadow Riderz were ordinary people who used what they had to help their neighbours. But someone took our story, changed our symbol, and used it to frighten young people."

Mara stood.

"I was part of that programme," she said. "I should have checked who owned the name and how the recruitment would affect the children. I have given the organisers my concerns, and the misleading campaign has been withdrawn."

Nia looked relieved.

"And the threatening messages?"

The safety officer explained that the messages had been referred for investigation. The public maintenance schedule had been used to make some of the threats seem mysterious. The exact sender of every message had not yet been confirmed.

The group listened.

They didn't need to invent a villain to feel better.

Some questions could remain open until there was evidence.

Then Biko looked toward Dantez.

"I heard you've made a decision about Guardian Grid."

Dantez stood.

"Yes."

He held up a simple paper plan.

**GEMA COMMUNITY DELIVERY TRACKER**

"It's a version of my old project," he said. "But this time it won't be for tricks or fake delivery receipts. Local schools and volunteers can choose to use it to organise donated books and materials. People must agree before their information is recorded."

Vanny Boy examined the drawing.

"The proposed operational architecture is considerably more aligned with legitimate community service."

Mdavido smiled.

"Good project."

"Precisely."

Labonator raised his hand.

"I also have a project."

Kariz sighed.

"Of course you do."

Labonator held up his machine.

"A vehicle safety reminder for the garage."

Kariz nodded.

"We can test it properly."

Lucanji raised his hand.

"I have a project too."

Everyone turned.

"What project?" Phylmyk asked.

"A biscuit delivery system."

Mdavido began laughing.

"How would that work?"

Lucanji looked puzzled.

"People give me biscuits. I deliver them to myself."

Even Kariz laughed.

Then Phylmyk stepped forward.

"I think the most important part is that we stop following strangers simply because they say we're special."

Dantez nodded.

He had learned that lesson the difficult way.

Biko stood and looked around the garage.

"Then perhaps the old riders can give the next generation something."

He opened the wooden box containing the original badge.

Not the fake black helmet emblem.

The real one.

Three roads.

Five riders.

One Gema.

He placed it on the table.

"This belongs to the history of the town," he said. "We don't need children riding motorcycles to honour it. We need young people who are willing to serve their community."

Dantez looked at Labonator.

Labonator looked at Dantez.

Then they both looked at Phylmyk.

She smiled.

"You're going to ask me to join something, aren't you?"

Labonator grinned.

"Not if you're going to arrest us."

"That depends."

"On what?"

"Whether you're planning another delivery from tomorrow."

Dantez laughed.

"No. This time, the deliveries will arrive on the correct day."

Mdavido gasped.

"THE WORLD IS HEALING!"

The garage erupted in laughter.

For the first time, Dantez felt proud without needing to defeat anyone.

He wasn't the smartest person in Gema.

He wasn't the most important.

He wasn't the chosen rider.

He was simply Dantez.

A boy who loved computers, motorcycles and building things.

And that was enough.

## Epilogue: The Phantom Garage

That evening, the sun sank behind the hills beyond Tamtam.

The workshop at Kwa Kariz was closing.

Labonator carefully parked his blue car.

Dantez shut down his laptop.

Kariz locked the storeroom.

Then the old garage telephone rang.

Kariz answered.

"Kwa Kariz. Who is speaking?"

Silence.

Then a voice said:

"You're looking after the wrong garage."

Kariz frowned.

"What?"

The voice continued.

"Ask Biko about the second workshop."

Then the call ended.

Kariz stood very still.

Dantez approached.

"What happened?"

Kariz looked toward the old photographs on the office wall.

"Nothing."

Labonator, approaching behind them, sighed.

"Every time somebody says nothing in this town, a new mystery begins."

Kariz didn't smile.

He removed a faded picture from the wall.

It showed an old corrugated-iron building beside the road to Shabe.

On the door was a painted number.

**ZERO.**

Dantez leaned closer.

"Where is that?"

Kariz hesitated.

"An old workshop. It was closed long before you were born."

"Who owned it?"

Kariz looked toward the dark road.

"Biko and another mechanic."

"What happened to it?"

Kariz slowly turned the photograph over.

On the back someone had written:

**GARAGE ZERO — DO NOT REOPEN.**

Dantez felt the familiar thrill of a new mystery.

Then a small black paper slid from behind the photograph and fell to the floor.

Labonator picked it up.

Three words were written across it.

**IT IS OPEN.**

For a moment, the garage was silent.

Then Labonator looked at Dantez.

"No."

Dantez smiled.

"Come on."

"No."

"We only need to ask Biko a question."

"That is how it starts!"

Dantez laughed.

Outside, a motorcycle passed beneath the streetlights.

This time neither boy followed it.

But both watched until the taillight vanished.

Somewhere beyond Shabe, a building that had stood empty for years had become important again.

And in Gema, secrets rarely stayed quiet for long.
